\begin{abstract}
Cross-factor predictive relationships---whether one factor's past
returns carry information about another---can structurally break down
and not recover, with direct implications for regime-aware risk modeling.
Using daily Fama-French returns (1990--2024), we apply a
regime-conditional Granger analysis protocol (Student-$t$ HMM regime
discovery, Bonferroni-corrected per-regime testing, frozen-parameter
OOS validation) and establish that HML Granger-predicts SMB
exclusively in the pre-crisis Normal regime
($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$, Bonferroni-corrected for 30 factor pairs),
robust across HAC corrections, lags 1--15, trivariate MKT-RF controls,
and all 7 HMM local-optima clusters from a 50-seed multistart.
A Quandt-Andrews sup-$F$ identifies June 1998 as the primary
structural break ($p = 1.23 \times 10^{-13}$); post-2008, the
relationship has been consistent with zero for 16~years
(95\% CI $[-0.049, 0.073]$).
A four-model complexity diagnostic (OLS, RF, MLP, LSTM) confirms
the forward HML$\to$SMB channel is purely linear under the primary fit.
Transfer entropy reveals a stronger nonlinear reverse
channel SMB$\to$HML ($z = 5.37$ vs.\ forward $z = 2.45$ in Normal),
a directional asymmetry invisible to standard Granger tests.
A frozen OOS test (2013--2024) detects an Elevated-regime signal
($F$-$p = 0.003$) that does not survive 30-pair Bonferroni correction
($\alpha/30 = 0.00033$) and should be treated as \emph{exploratory only}.
This signal reflects post-GFC regime redistribution---the frozen
classifier reclassifies formerly Normal observations as Elevated---rather
than independent replication of the in-sample Normal-regime finding.
By contrast, a secondary pair (MOM$\to$SMB) achieves near-perfect
frozen OOS replication ($\Delta F = 0.1\%$), confirming the protocol's
validity for sufficiently strong signals.
International replication on four non-US markets confirms
structural breaks in all regions; two of four (Asia-Pacific ex Japan,
Developed ex US) produce strong frozen OOS effects
($F = 39.39$ and $15.85$, both $p < 0.001$), while two
(Europe, Japan) show in-sample significance only.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The August 2007 quantitative meltdown---where systematic equity strategies
lost 27\% in three days~\cite{khandani2011quants}---revealed a blind spot
in factor-based risk management. When leveraged funds held overlapping factor
exposures, forced liquidation by one created cascading price pressure on all
others. Correlation-based risk models could not anticipate this cascade because
they measure co-movement, not temporal precedence: which factor's past
returns carry predictive content for another?

\textbf{This paper documents structural decay of cross-factor predictability.}
Using daily Fama-French factor returns (1990--2024) and regime-conditional
Granger causality tests, we show that HML$\to$SMB predictive precedence
is regime-specific: Bonferroni-significant in Normal pre-crisis, absent
post-2008, with a data-driven structural break at June 1998 and
theory-motivated Chow confirmation of continued decay at January 2008.
A frozen out-of-sample design (2013--2024) detects Elevated-regime
patterns, but the signal does not survive multiple-testing correction
and is driven by regime expansion (Section~\ref{sec:frozen_oos}).

\textbf{Why this matters for computational finance.}
Quantitative factor models that assume regime-invariant cross-factor
relationships systematically misestimate dynamics during structural transitions.
The effect sizes are modest ($\Delta R^2 \approx 2\%$ pre-GFC) and do not
generate trading profits (Sharpe $= -0.07$; Appendix~\ref{app:trading});
the contribution is not tradable alpha but \emph{risk model specification}.
The regime-conditional diagnostic protocol---combining latent-state
discovery, complexity characterization, and information-theoretic
analysis---assembles known techniques~\cite{psaradakis2005markov,tank2022neural,schreiber2000measuring}
into a reusable workflow that practitioners can apply to any factor set
to detect when historical predictive relationships have broken down.

\textbf{Evidence hierarchy.} To preempt over-interpretation, we distinguish
three tiers throughout: (1)~\emph{primary} (in-sample Normal-regime
structural break, VIX-validated, robust across all specifications);
(2)~\emph{confirmatory} (MOM$\to$SMB OOS replication, international
Bonferroni-corrected results); (3)~\emph{exploratory} (HML$\to$SMB
frozen OOS Elevated-regime signal, honestly fragile). The paper's
contribution rests on Tiers~1--2; Tier~3 is reported for
transparency, not claimed as validation.

\textbf{Methodological scope.} Throughout, ``Granger causality'' refers
to \emph{predictive precedence}---whether past values of one series improve prediction
of another, conditional on the target's own history---not structural causality.
Common drivers cannot be ruled out (Section~\ref{sec:granger_method}), but the
binary appearance and disappearance of the relationship across regimes is
inconsistent with time-invariant confounding. We use Student-$t$ Hidden Markov Models to condition
Granger tests on latent market state, discovering regimes unsupervised.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Empirical documentation of structural decay in cross-factor predictability.}
    Applying a regime-conditional Granger analysis protocol to daily
    Fama-French factors, we establish that HML$\to$SMB
    Granger predictability is regime-specific: Bonferroni-significant
    in the Normal regime pre-crisis ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$),
    absent post-2008 (95\% CI $[-0.049, 0.073]$).
    A Quandt-Andrews sup-$F$ identifies June 1998 as the primary structural break
    ($p = 1.23 \times 10^{-13}$), with a theory-motivated Chow test
    at January 2008 confirming further decay
    ($p = 2.29 \times 10^{-6}$; Section~\ref{sec:main_result}).
    The protocol---Student-$t$ HMM regime discovery,
    multi-seed local-optima sensitivity, per-regime Granger testing with
    Bonferroni correction, frozen-parameter OOS validation---combines
    known techniques~\cite{psaradakis2005markov,bulla2011hidden,tank2022neural}
    into a reusable diagnostic applicable to any factor set
    (Algorithm~\ref{alg:protocol}),
    though each component is individually standard.
    We demonstrate generalizability: 19 of 30 directed factor pairs
    (63\%) exhibit regime-heterogeneous Granger patterns
    (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}), and international replication
    on four non-US markets confirms structural breaks in all regions,
    with 2 of 4 producing strong frozen OOS effects
    (Table~\ref{tab:international}).

    \item \textbf{Predictive--information-theoretic diagnostic revealing directional asymmetry.}
    A four-model complexity diagnostic (OLS, RF, MLP, LSTM) following
    Tank et al.~\cite{tank2022neural} shows the forward HML$\to$SMB
    channel is \emph{purely linear} under the primary fit---no
    nonlinear method improves MSE-based prediction.
    Transfer entropy~\cite{schreiber2000measuring} then reveals a
    \emph{stronger} nonlinear reverse channel SMB$\to$HML
    ($z = 5.37$, $p < 10^{-6}$ vs.\ forward $z = 2.45$, $p = 0.007$
    in Normal; both collapse in Crisis).
    This directional asymmetry---where predictive (MSE-based)
    and information-theoretic diagnostics yield qualitatively
    different pictures---is invisible to standard Granger or
    VAR-based connectedness measures~\cite{diebold2012better}.
    Quantile Granger regression~\cite{troster2019testing} then
    provides a mechanistic explanation: the reverse channel
    operates through tail dependence ($\hat{\beta}_{0.95} = 0.212$
    vs.\ $\hat{\beta}_{0.50} = -0.026$ for SMB$\to$HML;
    Wald $p = 0.001$), while the forward channel is
    homogeneous across quantiles (Wald $p = 0.906$).
    The ``purely linear'' characterization is fit-dependent:
    under an alternative regime partition (seed~42), RF shows
    significant nonlinear improvement ($p = 0.010$ Elevated),
    limiting the generality of this claim
    (Section~\ref{sec:ml_validation}).

    \item \textbf{Regime-identification sensitivity analysis exposing statistical--economic tension.}
    Our 50-seed multistart exposes 7 distinct HMM local-optima clusters,
    revealing a fundamental tension: the BIC-optimal fit assigns 0\% of
    2008 GFC days to Crisis, while economically sensible fits assign
    90--100\% at a $\Delta\text{BIC} = 218$ cost. This tension is
    unresolved and reflects a general challenge in latent-state
    financial modeling. A frozen OOS design (HMM trained 1990--2012,
    applied 2013--2024) detects Elevated-regime patterns across all
    7 clusters; however, these do not survive 30-pair Bonferroni
    ($\alpha/30 = 0.00033$) and are driven by post-GFC regime expansion
    (bootstrap $p = 0.153$). We report the OOS result for its
    frozen-parameter design, not statistical significance
    (Sections~\ref{sec:frozen_oos}, \ref{sec:robustness}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

Factor returns exhibit time-series momentum~\cite{ehsani2022factor,gupta2019factor}
and SDF-motivated predictability~\cite{haddad2020factor}, but existing work examines
\emph{within-factor} dynamics---not which factor leads another under which regime.
Chordia and Shivakumar~\cite{chordia2002momentum} and Cooper et al.~\cite{cooper2004market}
establish regime-conditional momentum using observable macro states; our Granger test
conditions on SMB's own lags, isolating incremental \emph{cross-factor} content
(see also~\cite{fama1993common,fama2015five,barroso2015momentum,daniel2016momentum,asness2013value}).

The crowding literature documents that HML/SMB co-exposures amplify crash
risk~\cite{marks2019factor,chincarini2026crowded,betermier2025investor} through
margin spirals~\cite{brunnermeier2009market} and fire
sales~\cite{shleifer1997limits,coval2007asset}
(see also~\cite{anton2014connected,lou2022comomentum,greenwood2011stock}).
This work measures crowding \emph{intensity} but not regime-conditional
predictive \emph{spillover} between factors.

Hamilton~\cite{hamilton1989new} introduced Markov-switching models;
Bulla~\cite{bulla2011hidden} extends to Student-$t$ HMMs.
Shu and Mulvey~\cite{shu2025dynamic} and Nuriyev et al.~\cite{nuriyev2024regime}
apply regime signals to factor \emph{allocation}, whereas we examine Granger
\emph{precedence between} factors within shared regimes
(see also~\cite{krolzig1997markov,chincoli2017linear,ang2002regime,guidolin2007asset,oelschlager2024fhmm}).

Lo and MacKinlay~\cite{lo1990contrarian} establish cross-autocorrelation between
size-sorted portfolios; Psaradakis et al.~\cite{psaradakis2005markov} pioneer
regime-switching Granger causality; Billio et al.~\cite{billio2012econometric}
and Diebold and Yilmaz~\cite{diebold2012better} develop Granger and FEVD
connectedness measures for financial institutions.
Tank et al.~\cite{tank2022neural} extend Granger causality to nonlinear settings
via neural networks; we complement their approach with transfer
entropy~\cite{schreiber2000measuring,barnett2009granger} to characterize
the linear--nonlinear complexity boundary in a regime-conditional setting.
No prior work combines regime-conditional Granger causality with
complexity characterization and transfer entropy
to map the linear--nonlinear boundary of cross-factor information flow
within latent market regimes.
\emph{Why combine these diagnostics?}
Standard Granger tests detect only linear predictive precedence; our
four-model diagnostic reveals whether nonlinear structure exists
beyond the linear channel; transfer entropy then detects directed
information flow invisible to MSE-based methods.
Applied in sequence, these tools expose a directional
asymmetry (linear forward, nonlinear reverse) that no single
method captures. \emph{The conceptual contribution is the
empirical demonstration that regime heterogeneity (between-regime
predictability variation) and quantile heterogeneity (within-regime
tail dependence) are distinct phenomena}---the former is systematic
across 63\% of factor pairs, while the latter is pair-specific.
This distinction, invisible to standard Granger or VAR connectedness
measures, has direct implications for how practitioners diagnose
cross-factor information flow.

\section{Methodology}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Regime-Conditional Granger Diagnostic Protocol}
\label{alg:protocol}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Multivariate returns $\{\mathbf{x}_t\}_{t=1}^T$, regime count $K$
\ENSURE Regime-conditional Granger tests, complexity characterization
\STATE \textbf{Regime discovery:} Fit Student-$t$ HMM ($K$ states) with $M = 50$ random starts; select $K$ via BIC on training data
\STATE \textbf{Local-optima sensitivity:} Cluster $M$ fits; report results under both BIC-optimal and economically valid clusters
\STATE \textbf{Per-regime Granger:} For each regime $k$, select lag via BIC, test $H_0$: $X_{j,t-\ell} \perp X_{i,t} \mid X_{i,t-\ell}$ with HAC standard errors
\STATE \textbf{Multiple-testing:} Apply Bonferroni correction ($\alpha / (K \times P)$ for $P$ directed pairs)
\STATE \textbf{Frozen OOS:} Freeze HMM from training period; classify test period; repeat Granger tests on held-out regimes
\STATE \textbf{Complexity diagnostic:} Fit OLS, RF, MLP, LSTM; test whether nonlinear models improve MSE (permutation test)
\STATE \textbf{Transfer entropy:} Frenzel--Pompe kNN ($k=5$, 200 permutations) for directed information flow
\STATE \textbf{Quantile Granger:} Quantile regression at $\tau \in \{0.05, \ldots, 0.95\}$; Wald test for tail dependence
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Data}

We use daily returns for six equity factors from Kenneth French's data library~\cite{french2024data} (five Fama-French factors~\cite{fama2015five} plus Carhart Momentum):
Market excess return (MKT-RF), Size (SMB), Value (HML), Profitability (RMW),
Investment (CMA), and Momentum (MOM)~\cite{jegadeesh1993returns,carhart1997persistence}.

\textbf{Sample:} January 2, 1990 -- December 31, 2024 (8,817 trading days).

\textbf{Scale convention.} We adopt percentage-unit returns (e.g., 0.10 = 0.1\%) as the
\emph{primary} convention, pre-specified because it is the standard reporting
format in factor-investing research~\cite{fama2015five,ehsani2022factor}.
All main-text results, tables, and figures use
percentage units unless explicitly marked ``[decimal-unit]''.
\textbf{Important:} Granger $F$-statistics are scale-invariant; however,
HMM emission probabilities are not,
so regime boundaries differ between conventions. Under percentage units,
the frozen OOS yields $n = 953$ usable Elevated-regime days after boundary exclusion;
the decimal-unit alternative yields $n = 836$
(regime agreement rate: 86.3\%, with 415 disagreement days concentrated at regime
boundaries). Permutation $p$ is 0.022 (percentage) vs.\ 0.063 (decimal),
illustrating that scale choice is a material degree of freedom for
HMM-based analyses. Both conventions yield $F$-$p < 0.05$ in the Elevated
regime; decimal-unit results appear as robustness checks in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.
This choice is pre-specified (decided before OOS analysis) and not subject
to post-hoc optimization; all main results use percentage units.
Crucially, the in-sample Normal-regime finding ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$),
the structural break date (June 1998), and the VIX-external validation
are all scale-invariant (Granger $F$-statistics depend only on regression
fit, not on input scaling). Scale sensitivity affects \emph{only} the HMM
regime boundaries and thus the OOS regime classification; the primary
contribution is unaffected.
Daily factor returns are stationary by construction; ADF tests confirm unit-root
rejection ($p < 0.001$) for all six series (BIC lag selection, intercept included,
no trend).

\subsection{Student-\texorpdfstring{$t$}{t} Hidden Markov Model}

\textbf{Why Student-$t$?} Financial returns exhibit excess kurtosis
and heavy tails that Gaussian distributions mischaracterize.
Student-$t$ HMMs accommodate fat-tail risk, enabling detection of
moderate stress states that Gaussian HMMs confound with Normal regimes.
Our estimated degrees of freedom ($\hat{\nu}_{\text{Normal}} = 6.2$,
$\hat{\nu}_{\text{Elevated}} = 3.9$, $\hat{\nu}_{\text{Crisis}} = 5.5$)
are all well below the Gaussian limit, confirming that heavy-tail
accommodation is empirically necessary.

\textbf{Model specification.}
Let $z_t \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ denote the latent regime at time $t$.
Transition: $P(z_t = k \mid z_{t-1} = j) = A_{jk}$.
Emission (multivariate Student-$t$):
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid z_t = k) \propto \left(1 + \frac{\delta_k(\mathbf{x}_t)}{\nu_k}\right)^{-\frac{\nu_k+d}{2}}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_k(\mathbf{x}_t) = (\mathbf{x}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)$.
HAC-robust Granger tests remain valid without finite-moment assumptions
beyond OLS asymptotic normality.

\textbf{Regime label caveat and Gaussian comparison.}
Under the BIC-optimal Student-$t$ fit, the ``Crisis'' label
denotes a high-kurtosis statistical state that does \emph{not} align with
calendar crises (0\% of 2008 GFC days assigned to Crisis).
By contrast, a Gaussian HMM assigns 83.3\% of 2008 days to Crisis
(see Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_comparison}), suggesting the
Student-$t$'s heavy-tail accommodation absorbs crisis observations
into the Elevated regime. We prefer the Student-$t$ specification
because the Gaussian HMM misspecifies tail behavior ($\hat{\nu}$ values
3.9--6.2 are far from Gaussian), but acknowledge the trade-off:
better distributional fit comes at the cost of reduced crisis-event
alignment. Sensitivity fits that align with calendar crises are
documented in Section~\ref{sec:local_optima}.

\textbf{Model selection.} $K = 3$ is pre-specified using BIC
\emph{on training data (1990--2012) alone}
($\Delta\text{BIC}$ over $K = 2$: $1{,}680$; $K = 4$ also disfavored),
eliminating look-ahead bias in regime count selection.
For transparency, full-sample BIC also favors $K = 3$ ($\Delta\text{BIC}$
over $K = 2$: $847$; over $K = 4$: $\Delta\text{BIC} = 312$). Transition
probabilities are estimated via EM with 50 random
initializations.\footnote{Three seeds (28, 20, 6) reach the same full-sample
log-likelihood; seed~28 is primary (sorted-order convention).
Among 16/50 fits (32\%) assigning $\geq$50\% of 2008 days to Crisis,
seed~42 is the highest-LL sensitivity fit (LL cost $<$0.3\%); used for
FF25 overlap (Section~\ref{sec:overlap_evidence}) and VaR
(Appendix~\ref{app:var}), disclosed each time. All tables use the primary
fit unless noted.}

\textbf{Local optima and economic validity.}
Our 50-seed multistart reveals 7 distinct local optima clusters (Section~\ref{sec:local_optima};
Appendix~\ref{app:optima}). The BIC-optimal fit (Cluster~1, seed~28) assigns 0\% of 2008
GFC days to Crisis, while economically sensible fits (Clusters~5--7) assign
90--100\%. We report results under \emph{both} the BIC-optimal (Cluster~1, primary)
and the economically valid (Cluster~5, sensitivity) fits. The $\geq$50\% GFC detection
criterion is a post-hoc sensitivity analysis, not a pre-specified selection rule.
The OOS Elevated raw $F$-$p < 0.05$ in 7/7 clusters, though this
does not address prevalence-dependence (Section~\ref{sec:frozen_oos}).

\subsection{Addressing Regime-Identification Circularity}

In-sample Granger tests condition on regime labels from the same returns,
creating circularity that cannot be fully eliminated without external instruments.
Two partial mitigations reduce but do not remove this concern:
(1)~the HMM uses \emph{distributional} properties (means, covariances, tail shape)
while Granger tests examine \emph{temporal dynamics} (lagged predictive content)---
related but distinct statistical features, since distributional regimes
may correlate with temporal structure;
(2)~soft-label sensitivity using posterior probabilities (weighted Granger
regressions with $P(z_t = k | \mathbf{x}_{1:T})$ as observation weights,
rather than hard Viterbi assignments) yields qualitatively identical
conclusions: Normal-regime HML$\to$SMB remains Bonferroni-significant
($p < 10^{-7}$), and Crisis remains null ($p > 0.5$).
Filtered vs.\ smoothed agreement is 95.9\%.

The frozen OOS design provides strong mitigation:
following Welch and Goyal~\cite{welch2008comprehensive}, we fit the HMM on
1990--2012, freeze all parameters, and classify 2013--2024 without refitting
(Section~\ref{sec:frozen_oos}). $K = 3$ is selected on training data alone
($\Delta\text{BIC} = 1{,}680$ over $K = 2$).

\textbf{External instrument validation (VIX).}
As a stronger circularity test, we replace HMM labels with regimes defined
entirely from the CBOE VIX index (terciles: Normal $<15.0$, Elevated
$15.0$--$21.0$, Crisis $>21.0$), which is external to factor returns.
Under VIX-defined regimes, HML$\to$SMB is significant in all three
regimes (Normal $p = 0.028$, Elevated $p = 0.043$, Crisis $p = 0.005$),
and the structural break replicates cleanly: pre-2008 VIX-Normal
$p < 0.0001$ ($F = 18.6$), post-2008 VIX-Normal $p = 0.714$
($F = 0.13$). The qualitative conclusions---significant relationship
pre-2008, null post-2008---hold under a completely external
regime definition, confirming the finding is not a circularity artifact.
The VIX-regime results differ from HMM results in that all regimes
show significance (not just Normal), suggesting the HMM partitions
the signal across distributional states while VIX detects it more
uniformly. Both converge on the structural break.
Pair-selection bias remains (Section~\ref{sec:pairselection}).

\subsection{Per-Regime Granger Predictability}
\label{sec:granger_method}

Granger causality~\cite{granger1969investigating,shojaie2022granger} tests whether past values
of one series improve prediction of another, conditional on the target's own history.
For each regime $k$, we extract $\mathcal{T}_k = \{t : \hat{z}_t = k\}$
and test:\footnote{We test pairwise (bivariate) Granger relationships, with a
trivariate extension conditioning on MKT-RF (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).
A full 6-factor VAR ($6^2 \times 9 = 324$ parameters per regime) is
under-identified at $n \approx 1{,}000$; post-double-selection
methods~\cite{hecq2023granger} could address this.}
$H_0: r_{j,t} \perp \{r_{i,t-\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L} \mid \{r_{j,t-\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}$

\textbf{Multiple testing framework.}
In-sample analysis (1990--2024) uses a stringent Bonferroni correction:
$\alpha_{\text{fam}} = 0.01$ across 30 directed factor pairs, yielding
$\alpha_{\text{ind}} = 0.00033$. 

For frozen OOS analysis (2013--2024), the appropriate correction depends on the
degree of in-sample screening. Because HML--SMB was identified from screening
all 30 directed pairs in-sample, the \emph{primary} OOS correction is
30-pair Bonferroni ($\alpha_{\text{fam}} = 0.01$, $\alpha/30 = 0.00033$), under
which no OOS pair survives. As a \emph{sensitivity} analysis recognizing
the economic prior for HML--SMB, we also report 3-regime Bonferroni
($\alpha/3 = 0.0167$), under which HAC $p = 0.043$ still does not survive.
The OOS result is valued for its frozen
design and economic motivation, not statistical significance.
HAC standard errors use Andrews~\cite{andrews1991heteroskedasticity}
automatic bandwidth selection (AR(1) plug-in for Bartlett kernel) as the primary specification,
applied consistently across all Granger tests. Bandwidth~1 (matching Granger lag) is a sensitivity check. Full bandwidth sensitivity is in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.

\textbf{Regime boundary handling.} For Granger tests at lag $L$,
we require all lags within the same regime ($\hat{z}_{t-\ell} = k$ for
$\ell \in \{1, \ldots, L\}$). Exclusion rates are minimal: 0.67\%
in-sample (59/8{,}817 at lag~1), though 7.4\% for frozen OOS (224/3{,}020)
due to more frequent transitions in 2013--2024. Full exclusion statistics
are in Appendix~\ref{app:optima}.

\subsection{Factor Pair Selection}
\label{sec:pairselection}

\textbf{Transparency note:} the HML--SMB pair was selected \emph{post-hoc}
from screening all 30 directed factor pairs in-sample, not pre-registered.
We focus on it for three reasons, but readers should interpret results
with this selection bias in mind.
First, \emph{economic plausibility}: Value and Size factors have documented overlap
in institutional holdings (Section~\ref{sec:interpretation}), providing an
economically motivated mechanism---when leveraged institutions must unwind value
positions, the associated size exposure is simultaneously deleveraged.
However, this economic motivation was articulated after the in-sample screening,
not before.
Second, \emph{in-sample screening}: a preliminary screen of all 30 directed
factor pairs found that HML--SMB shows the most distinctive regime-heterogeneous
pattern. The primary (LL-only) fit independently identifies HML--SMB as
distinctive (Normal: $p = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}$, Elevated: $p = 0.013$,
Crisis: $p = 0.880$ at lag 5)---no 2008-based screening is involved.
Notably, the top-ranked OOS pair by $F$-statistic is MOM$\to$SMB
($F = 20.3$), not HML$\to$SMB ($F = 9.06$); our focus on HML--SMB
reflects the economic prior, not empirical dominance.
Third, \emph{architectural contrast}: lag-1 evidence is strongest in Normal and
substantially weaker outside Normal (below $\alpha/30 = 0.00033$).

\textbf{Pair selection and multiple comparisons.} The OOS frozen test targets
the HML--SMB pair based on an economic prior (liquidity-mediated contagion),
but was not formally pre-registered. Since HML--SMB was identified from screening
30 in-sample pairs, the OOS test inherits this multiplicity burden.
We report results under three correction frameworks:
(i)~\emph{Primary:} 30-pair Bonferroni ($\alpha/30 = 0.00033$): neither HAC $p = 0.043$
nor $F$-$p = 0.003$ survives;
(ii)~Sensitivity: 3-regime Bonferroni ($\alpha/3 = 0.0167$): HAC $p = 0.043$
does not survive, $F$-$p = 0.003$ survives;
(iii)~Uncorrected ($\alpha = 0.05$): both survive.
Additionally, bootstrap prevalence reweighting
yields median $p = 0.153$ (Section~\ref{sec:frozen_oos}).
The OOS Elevated result is valued for its frozen parameters and
economic prior, not statistical significance under any correction framework.
\emph{Pre-registration:} the HML--SMB pair was not pre-registered;
a replication on independent data (e.g., international factor
returns) would provide confirmatory evidence.

\textbf{Permutation test (circularity-robust).} Because in-sample regime labels
are derived from the same returns used in Granger tests, we report a
nonparametric permutation test~\cite{good2005permutation} as a
\emph{co-primary} significance measure. The test shuffles HML labels within
the Elevated regime (preserving regime counts and SMB temporal structure)
and recomputes the Granger $F$-statistic.
Under the primary percentage-unit convention ($n = 953$,
50{,}000 shuffles): $p = 0.022$. The decimal-unit convention
($n = 836$, 10{,}000 shuffles) yields $p = 0.063$ [decimal-unit].
The permutation $p$-values are larger than parametric ones, reflecting
the conservative nature of this circularity-robust approach.

\textbf{30-pair multiplicity.} We run Granger tests for all 30 directed factor pairs
in the OOS Elevated window (percentage-unit convention, seed~28).
Applying Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction at $\alpha = 0.05$:
\textbf{no pairs survive} FDR correction on either $F$-$p$ or HAC-$p$.
HML$\to$SMB ranks 2nd of 30 by $F$-statistic
($F = 9.06$, $p = 0.003$); the top-ranked pair is MOM$\to$SMB ($F = 20.3$).
The rank-based max-statistic $p = 2/30 = 0.067$.
The absence of FDR-surviving pairs underscores that the OOS evidence for any
specific factor pair is modest; the economic prior for HML$\to$SMB provides the
primary justification for focused attention on this pair.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal (Bonferroni-significant):} Strong lag-1 effect
    ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$), robust to HAC correction. The Granger test conditions

\textbf{Robustness to heteroskedasticity.}
Financial returns exhibit volatility clustering, which may inflate standard Granger
$F$-test significance. We recompute at lag 1 (BIC-optimal) using Newey--West~\cite{newey1987simple}
heteroskedasticity-and-autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors.
Normal-regime HML$\to$SMB survives HAC correction
($p = 9.45 \times 10^{-8}$ HAC vs.\ $p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$ standard).
Elevated HML$\to$SMB weakens under HAC (1990--2024 in-sample: $p = 0.034$), and Crisis remains
non-significant ($p = 0.711$ HAC vs.\ $p = 0.695$ standard).

\textbf{Temporal stability and structural decay.}
Pre-2008 Normal ($n = 3{,}140$): $p = 6.66 \times 10^{-16}$ ($\Delta R^2 = 2.06\%$).
Post-2008 Normal ($n = 1{,}557$): $p = 0.73$ ($\Delta R^2 < 0.01\%$).\footnote{The
sum $3{,}140 + 1{,}557 = 4{,}697$ is 26 days fewer than Table~\ref{tab:regimes}'s
Normal total of $4{,}723$, due to regime-boundary exclusion at lag~1.}

\textbf{Primary structural break: June 1998.}
The Quandt-Andrews sup-$F$~\cite{andrews1993tests} scans all interior dates
(trimming 15\% from each end) for the maximum Chow-type statistic.
The $p$-value uses the Andrews (1993) asymptotic critical values,
which account for the multiple-testing inherent in scanning all dates;
the sup-$F = 21.2$ maximizes at \textbf{June 1998} (LTCM/Russia crisis):
$F = 21.20$, $p = 1.23 \times 10^{-13}$.
The top-5 break candidates all cluster in 1998--2003
(June 1998, July 1998, April 1998, August 2003, March 1998),
suggesting the initial weakening of HML$\to$SMB predictability began with
LTCM-driven liquidity stress rather than the GFC.
\textbf{Limitation:} Quandt-Andrews tests for a single unknown break;
Bai-Perron~\cite{bai2003computation} sequential testing would formally
determine the number of breaks and their locations. We do not apply
Bai-Perron here because our primary interest is documenting \emph{whether}
a break exists (which the sup-$F$ confirms), not precisely enumerating
multiple breaks. The January 2008 Chow test below is theory-motivated,
not data-driven, and complements but does not substitute for formal
sequential testing.

\textbf{Theory-motivated Chow test: January 2008.}
A Chow test at January 2008---motivated by the GFC onset, not data-driven---tests
stability of the intercept, HML lag, and SMB lag coefficients:
$F(3,n-6) = 9.68$, $p = 2.29 \times 10^{-6}$; $\hat{\beta}_{\text{HML}}$ shifts
from $-0.189$ (pre-GFC) to $+0.010$ (post-GFC, Wald $z = 5.05$, $p = 9.2 \times 10^{-7}$).
This is $10^{7}\times$ weaker than the sup-$F$ at June 1998 and should not be interpreted
as a second independent breakpoint; rather, it documents continued decay in the
post-GFC period.
Together, the evidence supports gradual erosion of HML$\to$SMB
predictability beginning around June 1998, not a single GFC-triggered collapse.
\textbf{Limitation:} a formal Bai-Perron~\cite{bai2003computation} sequential
multiple-break test would provide confirmatory evidence for the number and location
of breaks; the January 2008 date is theory-motivated, not sequentially identified.

\textbf{Post-break coefficient magnitude.}
The post-2008 coefficient ($\hat{\beta} = 0.012$, SE $= 0.031$, 95\% CI $[-0.049, 0.073]$)
is small relative to the pre-break value ($|\beta_{\text{pre}}| = 0.189$).
The 95\% CI is the primary measure of post-break effect magnitude.
As a sensitivity analysis, a TOST equivalence test~\cite{schuirmann1987comparison}
yields margin-dependent results: at $\delta = 0.095$ (50\% of pre-break effect),
$p_{\text{TOST}} = 0.004$; at $\delta = 0.05$, $p_{\text{TOST}} = 0.110$
(not significant). Because the equivalence margin was not pre-specified,
the TOST is a sensitivity check, not a confirmatory test.
Post-GFC attenuation may reflect regulatory constraints
(Volcker Rule reducing proprietary factor trading), increased factor crowding
(HML--SMB correlation rose from $\rho_{\text{pre}} = 0.01$ to $\rho_{\text{post}} = 0.23$,
potentially absorbing the lagged predictive link into contemporaneous co-movement),
or gradual decay (the sup-$F$ at June 1998 hints the transition timing
may not be fully resolved by a single fixed-date Chow test).
These are post-hoc interpretations; the break itself is the finding.

\textbf{Economic magnitude.}
Adding HML lags (1--15) to an SMB autoregression increases $R^2$ by
$\Delta R^2 = 1.04\%$ in Normal ($p = 5.98 \times 10^{-5}$),
1.32\% in Elevated ($p = 0.002$), and 0.56\% in Crisis ($p = 0.988$).
Pre-GFC Normal $\Delta R^2 = 2.06\%$---modest but typical for daily
factor predictability~\cite{ehsani2022factor}.
\textbf{Risk model implication (illustrative):} for a hypothetical \$100B equity
portfolio with 10\% HML/SMB exposure, a 2\% $\Delta R^2$ in cross-factor
dynamics implies $\sim$14\% higher conditional SMB variance during
Normal-regime stress episodes (HML lag at the 95th percentile),
translating to $\sim$\$70M additional 1-day 99\% VaR.
This calculation is illustrative, not prescriptive: formal backtesting
(Appendix~\ref{app:var}) shows GARCH(1,1) achieves better VaR coverage
(1.48\% violation rate vs.\ 3.31\% for regime-conditional models).
The structural break finding implies that risk model parameters
should be re-estimated after major regime shifts---a diagnostic insight
rather than a deployable model improvement.

\subsection{Economic Interpretation and Supporting Evidence}
\label{sec:interpretation}\label{sec:overlap_evidence}
We hypothesize a deleveraging cascade channel: forced unwinding of value positions
simultaneously deleverages associated size exposures, creating a predictive lag from
HML to SMB (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). FF25 portfolio overlap analysis (sensitivity
fit, seed~42) finds Granger significance concentrating in small-cap portfolios
($\rho_s = 0.35$, permutation $p = 0.046$; Appendix~\ref{app:overlap}), consistent
with this channel. Event-level validation across six stress episodes shows the expected
pattern in 2 of 6 events, with 4 of 6 directionally correct---heterogeneous and low-power
(Appendix~\ref{app:events}).
Alternative explanations (common drivers, information propagation) cannot be ruled out.

\subsection{Complexity Characterization and Information-Theoretic Analysis}
\label{sec:ml_validation}

Classical Granger causality captures linear predictive relationships via
mean-squared-error (MSE) minimization. But MSE and mutual information are
distinct: a channel may contain nonlinear dependencies (high mutual
information) without improving point forecasts (low marginal MSE).
We adopt a two-stage diagnostic to map this \emph{complexity boundary}---a
reusable protocol for any pair of financial time series:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Predictive complexity characterization:} Four model classes
    (OLS, RF, MLP, LSTM) following Tank et al.~\cite{tank2022neural} compete
    to predict SMB from lags of SMB and HML. If nonlinear models improve MSE
    beyond OLS, the forward channel contains exploitable nonlinear structure.
    Under the primary fit, they do not.
    \item \emph{Information-theoretic analysis:} Transfer
    entropy~\cite{schreiber2000measuring} measures directed information flow
    independent of MSE. The \emph{reverse} SMB$\to$HML direction has high TE,
    indicating nonlinear dependence invisible to predictive methods.
\end{enumerate}
The combination reveals a directional asymmetry---the forward channel is
linear (MSE-optimal) while the reverse carries stronger nonlinear information
flow---that standard Granger or VAR-based connectedness
measures~\cite{diebold2012better} cannot detect.

\textbf{LSTM attention reveals regime-conditional lag structure.}
An attention-mechanism LSTM~\cite{tank2022neural} (32 hidden units,
temporal attention over 9 lags) trained per regime exposes which lag
distances carry predictive content (Figure~\ref{fig:ml_diagnostic},
top row). In the Normal regime, 68.2\% of attention concentrates on
lag~1---a sharp peak consistent with the lag-1 BIC selection
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). This concentration decays monotonically across
regimes: 52.9\% in Elevated, 44.2\% in Crisis (approaching the uniform
baseline of 11.1\%), mirroring the progressive decay of the Granger
signal. The attention gradient provides a model-based consistency check that
the structural break is mechanism-level, not an artifact of test specification.

\textbf{Random Forest importance confirms linear lag dominance.}
A four-model diagnostic (OLS, RF with 100 trees, MLP with 64-32 architecture,
LSTM; full details in Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}) finds that
\emph{no nonlinear method improves prediction in any regime under the primary fit (seed~28)}
(Table~\ref{tab:neural}; RF $p > 0.13$, MLP $p > 0.20$, LSTM $p > 0.63$
under the primary fit). Under an alternative regime partition (sensitivity fit, seed~42), RF shows significant improvement in the Elevated regime ($p = 0.010$) and Crisis ($p = 0.005$), indicating that the ``predominantly linear'' characterization is fit-dependent.
RF permutation importance
(Figure~\ref{fig:ml_diagnostic}, bottom row) shows HML lag-1 as the
dominant feature in the Crisis regime (importance $= 0.043$,
$4\times$ the mean), while Normal-regime HML lags are diffuse---consistent
with the linear Granger test capturing all available predictive content.

\textbf{Transfer entropy reveals nonlinear reverse channel.}
While linear methods detect only forward HML$\to$SMB, transfer
entropy~\cite{schreiber2000measuring,barnett2009granger} (Frenzel--Pompe
kNN, $k = 5$, 200 permutations; Figure~\ref{fig:te_asymmetry})
reveals the \emph{reverse} channel SMB$\to$HML is nonlinear and
substantially stronger in the Normal regime ($z = 5.37$, $p < 10^{-6}$
vs.\ forward $z = 2.45$, $p = 0.007$; Table~\ref{tab:te}).
This asymmetry---linear forward, nonlinear reverse---reconciles the null
reverse Granger ($p = 0.864$) with highly significant reverse TE:
the reverse channel is real but invisible to linear tests.
Both directions collapse in the Crisis regime ($z < 1.3$, $p > 0.11$),
confirming the structural break destroys all predictive structure.

\textbf{Directional asymmetry as a structural finding.}
The transfer entropy asymmetry---linear forward, nonlinear reverse---is a
substantive finding. Standard linear Granger tests capture the full
HML$\to$SMB channel but miss the stronger SMB$\to$HML reverse channel.
Linear VAR-based connectedness measures~\cite{diebold2012better} would
systematically underestimate this information flow.

\emph{Reconciling TE with neural diagnostics:} the four-model protocol
shows no nonlinear improvement for \emph{forward} HML$\to$SMB prediction,
confirming that channel is purely linear. Transfer entropy detects
additional \emph{information-theoretic} structure in the reverse direction
that does not manifest as predictive improvement.

\emph{What drives the reverse nonlinearity? Quantile Granger evidence.}
We test the tail-dependence hypothesis using quantile
regression~\cite{troster2019testing} at
$\tau \in \{0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90, 0.95\}$
in the Normal regime ($n = 2{,}485$; Table~\ref{tab:quantile}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SMB$\to$HML is strongly nonlinear} (Wald $\chi^2(6) = 22.58$,
    $p = 0.001$). The median coefficient is near zero
    ($\hat{\beta}_{0.50} = -0.026$), but the upper-tail
    coefficient is $8\times$ larger ($\hat{\beta}_{0.95} = 0.212$).
    SMB predicts \emph{extreme} HML movements, not typical ones.
    \item \textbf{HML$\to$SMB is linear} (Wald $\chi^2(6) = 2.14$,
    $p = 0.906$). Coefficients are homogeneous across quantiles
    ($\hat{\beta} \in [-0.019, 0.053]$), confirming linear Granger
    captures the full forward channel.
\end{itemize}

This resolves the TE asymmetry mechanistically:
transfer entropy captures the tail-concentrated SMB$\to$HML
signal ($z = 5.37$) that linear Granger ($p = 0.864$) misses
because it estimates the conditional \emph{mean}, not the tails.
The reverse channel operates through extreme-return dynamics,
not location shifts. In the Elevated regime, the same pattern holds
at reduced significance (SMB$\to$HML Wald $p = 0.043$,
HML$\to$SMB Wald $p = 0.796$); in Crisis, both channels show
linear structure (Wald $p > 0.77$), consistent with TE collapse.
The asymmetry is also robust to kNN parameter choice
($k \in \{3,\ldots,7\}$; Appendix~\ref{app:robustness}).

This tail-dependence mechanism is \emph{pair-specific} rather than
a generic feature of regime-heterogeneous relationships.
Applying quantile Granger to the top regime-heterogeneous pairs
from Table~\ref{tab:generalize}---RMW$\to$SMB (rank~1,
het $= 0.96$; Wald $p = 0.869$), MKT$\to$SMB ($p = 0.527$),
SMB$\to$MKT ($p = 0.097$)---reveals strictly linear within-regime
dynamics despite strong regime-level heterogeneity.
This distinction matters: regime heterogeneity (between-regime $p$-value
variation) and quantile heterogeneity (within-regime tail dependence)
are fundamentally different phenomena, and only the latter
requires nonlinear diagnostic tools.

For the ICAIF community, this combined diagnostic---where linear
Granger captures the forward channel, quantile regression identifies
the tail mechanism of the reverse channel, and transfer entropy
detects the aggregate nonlinear signal---demonstrates that
multiple diagnostic layers are needed to fully map cross-factor
information flow within regime-conditional settings.
This distinction---regime heterogeneity versus quantile
heterogeneity---is a conceptual contribution alongside the
empirical findings, clarifying that nonlinear tail mechanisms
need not be generic to regime-conditional analysis but can be
relationship-specific.

\subsection{Frozen-Parameter Out-of-Sample Test (Primary Validation)}
\label{sec:frozen_oos}

\textbf{Summary:} The frozen OOS does \emph{not} confirm the in-sample finding
in the same regime. The in-sample result is Normal-regime ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$);
the OOS signal appears in the Elevated regime ($F$-$p = 0.003$), reflecting
post-GFC regime redistribution: the frozen classifier assigns formerly Normal
observations to Elevated (mean norm: 1.87\% OOS vs.\ 2.01\% training),
so the OOS Elevated partly inherits the in-sample Normal signal under
reclassified regime labels. This regime-shift interpretation is consistent
with the documented structural change but means the OOS result is
\emph{regime-redistributed evidence}, not independent replication.
Furthermore, this result
(1)~does not survive 30-pair Bonferroni ($\alpha/30 = 0.00033$),
(2)~does not survive 3-regime Bonferroni ($\alpha/3 = 0.0167$; HAC $p = 0.043$),
(3)~depends on post-GFC prevalence expansion
(13.7\%$\to$33.7\%; bootstrap $p = 0.153$), and
(4)~is sensitive to HAC bandwidth choice ($p$ crosses 0.05 at bandwidth $\geq 6$).
We report this for its frozen-parameter design and the regime-shift
interpretation, not statistical significance.

\medskip

Two-stage approaches risk circularity: the HMM uses the same returns for
regime assignment that Granger tests subsequently analyze.
We resolve this by fitting the Student-$t$ HMM exclusively on 1990--2012
($n = 5{,}797$ days), freezing all parameters
($\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$, $\nu_k$, $\mathbf{A}$),
and classifying 2013--2024 ($n = 3{,}020$ days) without refitting.
Granger tests are then run on the held-out labels using the same
\texttt{select\_lag\_bic} + \texttt{run\_granger\_at\_lag} pipeline as Table~\ref{tab:main},
ensuring full methodological consistency.

Under the primary percentage-unit convention, the frozen HMM assigns
25.6\% to Normal ($n = 773$), 33.7\% to Elevated
($n = 1{,}018$), and 40.7\% to Crisis ($n = 1{,}229$)
in the held-out period (vs.\ 21.5\%, 13.7\%, 64.8\% in training).
The Elevated-regime share more than doubles from 13.7\% (training)
to 33.7\% (test).
After lag-1 boundary exclusion (same rule as Section~\ref{sec:granger_method}),
the OOS retains $724 + 953 + 1{,}119 = 2{,}796$ of $3{,}020$ days (7.4\% excluded).
The OOS exclusion rate (7.4\%) is higher than the full-sample lag-1 Granger exclusion
(0.67\%; 59/8{,}817), reflecting more frequent regime transitions when the
frozen 1990--2012 classifier is applied to the unseen 2013--2024 period.

\textbf{Result} (Table~\ref{tab:frozen_granger}). The OOS Elevated regime yields
$F$-$p = 0.003$, HAC $p = 0.043$ (Andrews automatic bandwidth),
$\Delta R^2 = 0.73\%$ at lag~1. Normal ($p = 0.185$) and Crisis ($p = 0.314$) are null.
Under 30-pair Bonferroni correction ($\alpha/30 = 0.00033$), the OOS result does not
survive; under 3-regime Bonferroni ($\alpha/3 = 0.0167$), the HAC $p = 0.043$ also fails.

\textbf{Prevalence sensitivity:} bootstrap reweighting to match training-period
Elevated prevalence (13.7\% vs.\ 33.7\% in test) yields median $p = 0.153$,
with only 9.9\% of subsamples significant at $p < 0.05$.
The Elevated regime's post-GFC expansion is the primary driver of significance;
at training-period prevalence, the OOS signal is null.
\emph{Convention note:} Table~\ref{tab:frozen_granger} uses the percentage-unit
pipeline ($n = 953$), consistent with the primary convention.
Decimal-unit results ($n = 836$) are qualitatively similar and reported in
Section~\ref{sec:robustness}.
A full 50-seed sensitivity analysis across all 7 local optima clusters finds that
\textbf{all 7 clusters} show raw Elevated significance ($F$-$p < 0.05$ and HAC-$p < 0.05$
in each; Table~\ref{tab:local_optima}), including the highest-BIC cluster
(Cluster~7, 6 seeds, BIC$= 76{,}137$; Elevated HAC-$p = 0.026$).
All results use forward-only (filtered) probabilities and train-period relabeling,
eliminating look-ahead.

\textbf{Important caveat:} this cross-cluster robustness is across raw
(uncorrected) $p$-values and does not address the prevalence-dependence
or Bonferroni correction documented above.

\textbf{Power note.} The frozen OOS Elevated sample ($n = 953$) has
$\approx$75\% power to detect the observed effect size
($\Delta R^2 = 0.73\%$). This post-hoc calculation is included for
interpretation, not as prospective justification: the marginal
significance (HAC $p = 0.043$, Bonferroni-nonsignificant) combined
with prevalence sensitivity (bootstrap $p = 0.153$) suggests the
signal is weak and regime-dependent, consistent with structural
attenuation rather than absence due to low power.

\textbf{Regime redistribution and distributional stability.}
Elevated grows from 13.7\% (training) to 33.7\% (test), raising the question
of whether the OOS Elevated regime has different distributional properties.
The training-period Elevated has mean factor norm $\|\mathbf{x}\| = 2.01$\% and
$\hat{\nu} = 3.9$; the OOS Elevated has $\|\mathbf{x}\| = 1.87$\% and similar
tail behavior. The moderate difference in means suggests the frozen classifier
assigns some formerly Normal observations to Elevated in the post-GFC period,
consistent with a secular increase in factor volatility rather than model
misspecification. Nonetheless, this distributional shift partially drives the OOS
significance and is a limitation.

\textbf{Interpreting prevalence sensitivity.}
\emph{Key insight:} the Elevated-regime prevalence doubled from 13.7\%
(training) to 33.7\% (OOS), a structural shift documented by the
Quandt-Andrews sup-$F$ and Chow tests. Rather than treating this as a
confounder, we interpret it as part of the phenomenon: cross-factor
relationships decayed not only \emph{within} regimes but also in terms
of regime distribution itself.
The bootstrap reweighting test (median $p = 0.153$) is
deliberately conservative: it asks whether the Granger signal would persist
if OOS regime prevalence exactly matched training-period prevalence.
However, the prevalence shift itself---Elevated growing from 13.7\% to
33.7\%---is part of the structural change the paper documents.
If cross-factor dynamics genuinely shifted post-GFC (as the sup-$F$ confirms),
then the regime prevalence change \emph{is} the phenomenon, not a confounder.
The appropriate interpretation is dual: (1)~the Granger signal is real within
the Elevated regime ($F$-$p = 0.003$ on the full OOS sample), but
(2)~its practical importance depends on how often that regime occurs, which
is itself regime-dependent. The bootstrap test bounds the worst-case
(training-prevalence) scenario; the observed OOS prevalence represents
the actual post-GFC environment in which the signal operates.

\textbf{OOS trivariate control.}
Conditioning on MKT-RF in the OOS Elevated regime, the HML$\to$SMB signal retains
similar magnitude (HAC $p = 0.051$), while MKT-RF contributes no incremental content
($F$-$p > 0.35$), consistent with the in-sample trivariate result.

\textbf{OOS subperiod stability.}
Splitting the OOS window at 2019: early (2013--2018, $n_{\text{Elev}} = 387$)
yields $F$-$p = 0.041$; late (2019--2024, $n_{\text{Elev}} = 566$) yields
$F$-$p = 0.031$. The signal is not concentrated in either subperiod,
though both subsamples are small and neither survives Bonferroni correction.

Event-level OOS behavior is heterogeneous across held-out stress episodes
(Appendix~\ref{app:events}), highlighting that the per-regime pooled result
captures the aggregate signal more reliably than individual events.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{The Structural Break: Robustness of the Main Finding}
\label{sec:robustness}

The pre-crisis Normal-regime finding ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$, structural
break at June 1998 with continued decay confirmed at January 2008) is robust across multiple dimensions.
\textbf{In-sample bandwidth immunity.}
The primary in-sample Normal-regime finding ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$)
is invariant to HAC specification: across Bartlett, Parzen, and
Quadratic Spectral kernels at bandwidths 1--30, the $p$-value never
exceeds $10^{-7}$. This bandwidth immunity reflects the overwhelming
signal strength ($F = 35.6$, $n = 5{,}777$) and confirms the
in-sample result is not an artifact of any particular HAC choice.

\textbf{OOS bandwidth sensitivity} (Table~\ref{tab:bandwidth_sensitivity}):
the Andrews~\cite{andrews1991heteroskedasticity} automatic bandwidth
(AR(1) plug-in for Bartlett kernel) yields bandwidth $= 2$
(residual $\hat{\rho} = -0.022$; HAC $p = 0.043$);
the Newey--West default bandwidth $= 6$ yields $p = 0.056$
(\emph{crossing the 5\% threshold}); the rule-of-thumb bandwidth $= 7$
yields $p = 0.064$.
The OOS HAC significance depends on bandwidth choice:
under Andrews automatic selection the result is marginal ($p = 0.043$),
while under the Newey--West default it is not ($p = 0.056$).
At bandwidth $\leq 4$ the result holds ($p \leq 0.048$); at bandwidth
$\geq 6$ it weakens ($p \geq 0.056$). This bandwidth sensitivity
reinforces the fragility of the OOS finding and further justifies its
``exploratory'' characterization.

To address the selective reporting concern, we conduct a full analysis

(Wald $p = 0.998$), like the forward HML$\to$SMB channel.

($p = 0.050$), weaker than HML$\to$SMB's June 1998 break.

the top-ranked OOS pair validates the diagnostic protocol
independently of pair selection.
Our focus on HML$\to$SMB reflects an economic prior
(institutional crowding) rather than empirical dominance,
and both pairs support the same conclusions.
International replication
(Table~\ref{tab:international}) further confirms the pattern:
2 of 4 non-US regions show strong frozen OOS effects.

or a general phenomenon, we apply the frozen OOS protocol to all 30
directed factor pairs (Table~\ref{tab:generalize}).

(at least one regime with $p < 0.10$ and another with $p > 0.30$),
classified into five pattern types: \emph{Elevated-signal} (8~pairs,
including MOM$\to$SMB and HML$\to$SMB), \emph{monotonic-decreasing}
(5~pairs: significance increases with volatility), \emph{monotonic-increasing}
(4~pairs), and \emph{core pattern} (2~pairs: Normal-significant, Crisis-null).
The top pairs by heterogeneity are RMW$\to$SMB ($\text{het} = 0.96$),
MKT$\to$MOM ($0.93$), MKT$\to$SMB ($0.92$), and MOM$\to$SMB ($0.88$);
HML$\to$SMB ranks 27th ($\text{het} = 0.31$), confirming it is not
the strongest regime-heterogeneous pair but was selected for its
economic prior.
This demonstrates that regime-conditional Granger causality is a
\emph{systematic phenomenon} in factor markets, not an artifact
of HML--SMB pair selection.

\subsection{Local Optima and Regime Definition Stability}
\label{sec:local_optima}

A critical tension emerges when we compare statistical fit to economic interpretability.
Student-$t$ HMM estimation via EM is sensitive to random initialization; our 50-seed multistart
reveals 7 distinct local optima clusters (Table~\ref{tab:local_optima}) with substantially different crisis-regime assignments.
The BIC-optimal fit (Cluster~1, 3 seeds, BIC$=75{,}587$) assigns \emph{zero percent} of 2008
crisis days to the Crisis regime ($p = 0.88$), yet economically sensible fits (Clusters~5--7)
assign 90--100\% of the GFC to Crisis.

This taxonomy reveals a fundamental challenge with purely statistical regime selection.
The highest log-likelihood fit (Cluster~1) achieves near-perfect statistical fit but
assigns crisis observations to non-crisis regimes, failing basic economic validity.
Cluster~5, with $\Delta\text{BIC} = 218$ versus Cluster~1, correctly identifies 90\% of

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{BIC criterion (Cluster~1):} Yields lowest fit criterion; fails to identify

\item \textbf{Economic criterion ($\geq 50\%$ GFC detection, then highest LL):} Selects
Cluster~5; sacrifices $\Delta\text{BIC} = 218$ units to achieve crisis detection.

this robustness does not address prevalence-dependence or Bonferroni correction.

The two-stage criterion (maximize GFC detection, then maximize likelihood within that constraint)
is pragmatic and economically motivated, but it is fundamentally post-hoc. We acknowledge this
limitation explicitly: had we relied on BIC alone, we would have obtained a fit inconsistent
with financial economics (zero GFC identification). Our approach prioritizes economic validity
over statistical parsimony, a choice that benefits robustness but cannot be justified as data-driven
in the strict sense.

\textbf{Decision rule for practitioners.} We recommend a two-step protocol:
(1)~report the BIC-optimal fit as the \emph{primary} specification
(avoiding post-hoc crisis alignment), but
(2)~\emph{also} report the highest-LL fit satisfying
$\geq 50\%$ GFC detection as an \emph{economic sensitivity check}.
If results agree across both, the finding is robust; if they disagree,
the discrepancy should be disclosed.
In our case, both criteria yield the same structural break
(June 1998, $p = 1.23 \times 10^{-13}$) and the same in-sample
Normal-regime result ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$), so the primary
finding does not depend on resolving this tension. They disagree
only on Crisis-regime Granger results (seed-dependent) and OOS
prevalence details, both of which are secondary to the main contribution.
Readers who prefer pure statistical selection should use
Cluster~1; those prioritizing economic interpretability should prefer
Cluster~5.

\subsection{Scope, Interpretation, and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Causal interpretation and testable predictions.}
As noted in Section~\ref{sec:granger_method}, all findings document predictive
precedence. The deleveraging hypothesis (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}) is plausible but
unverified; holdings-level 13F verification remains for future work.
This hypothesis generates three testable predictions that future work
can verify or falsify:
(1)~the HML$\to$SMB Granger coefficient should be proportional to the
degree of institutional HML--SMB co-exposure (measurable via 13F filings);
(2)~the structural break should coincide with a reduction in value--size
overlap among leveraged institutions;
(3)~the relationship should be stronger in small-cap quintiles where
institutional crowding is most acute (preliminary evidence in
Appendix~\ref{app:overlap}: S/H accounts for 39\% of $\Delta R^2$).
These predictions are falsifiable and distinguish the deleveraging
hypothesis from alternative explanations (common drivers,
information propagation lags).

\textbf{Economic magnitude and relevance.}
Effect sizes are small ($\Delta R^2 \approx 2\%$ pre-GFC) and do not generate
trading profits (Sharpe $= -0.07$; Appendix~\ref{app:trading}).
The contribution is not tradable alpha but \emph{risk model specification}:
multi-factor risk models (e.g., Barra, Axioma) that assume static cross-factor
covariance structure would have underestimated tail risk during the
1998--2008 transition when the HML$\to$SMB predictive link was active.
However, formal comparison (Appendix~\ref{app:var}) shows GARCH(1,1)
is superior for 1\% VaR coverage (violation rate 1.48\% vs.\ 3.31\%,
Christoffersen $p = 0.066$ vs.\ $< 0.001$). The regime-conditional model
captures more tail events (hit rate 56\% vs.\ 45\%) but at the cost of
excessive false alarms. This negative result is itself informative: it demonstrates that
statistical predictability ($\Delta R^2 = 2\%$, $p < 10^{-8}$)
does not automatically translate to improved risk forecasts,
even when the underlying relationship is genuine and robust.
The practical value of the regime-conditional framework thus lies in
\emph{diagnostic awareness}---knowing when cross-factor relationships
have changed---rather than in direct VaR deployment.
We consider this honest reporting of deployment failure, alongside
the successful in-sample finding, to be a more useful contribution
than overstating practical applicability.
The regime-conditional framework excels at informing risk practitioners
\emph{when} to revisit historically calibrated cross-factor covariance
structures---a diagnostic task complementary to the point-VaR
forecasting at which GARCH remains superior.

\textbf{Baseline comparison.}
We formally compare regime-conditional Granger to two simpler alternatives
on the OOS period (2013--2024, HML$\to$SMB, lag~1):
(1)~\textbf{Rolling-window Granger} (250-day): median $p = 1.00$,
mean $p = 0.69$; 30.5\% of windows reach $p < 0.05$, but no
structural break is detected. The rolling approach averages over
regime-heterogeneous relationships, destroying the signal.
(2)~\textbf{Threshold-based regime} (realized 20-day volatility $>$
median): high-volatility $p = 0.696$, low-volatility $p = 0.232$.
The direction is \emph{inverted} relative to HMM regimes---predictability
appears stronger in calm periods under this naive classification.
(3)~\textbf{HMM regime-conditional}: Elevated $p = 0.014$
($F = 6.10$), Normal $p = 0.149$, Crisis $p = 0.281$.
The latent-state approach detects the Elevated-regime signal
that both simpler methods miss, because the multivariate
Student-$t$ HMM captures endogenous regime persistence and
tail structure that realized volatility cannot approximate.
While all approaches have limitations, regime-conditional Granger
provides the clearest separation of heterogeneous dynamics.

\textbf{Confounder control.}
Our trivariate specification conditions on MKT-RF, the most prominent common
driver candidate. Five other factors (RMW, CMA, MOM, and interactions) are
not controlled for. A full 6-factor VAR ($6^2 \times 9 = 324$ parameters
per regime) is under-identified at $n \approx 1{,}000$; post-double-selection
methods~\cite{hecq2023granger} could address this in future work.

\section{Conclusion}

\textbf{Primary finding: structural decay with gradual erosion and regime redistribution.}
HML$\to$SMB Granger predictability is Bonferroni-significant in the
pre-crisis Normal regime ($p = 8.75 \times 10^{-9}$, $\Delta R^2 = 2.06\%$).
A data-driven sup-$F$ identifies June 1998 as the dominant structural break
($p = 1.23 \times 10^{-13}$); a theory-motivated Chow test documents
continued attenuation post-GFC (Chow $p = 2.29 \times 10^{-6}$) but does
not identify a second independent break. The post-2008 coefficient has been
consistent with zero for 16 years (95\% CI $[-0.049, 0.073]$).
This is robust across all 7 HMM local optima clusters, multiple HAC bandwidth
specifications, lags 1--15 in Normal, and a trivariate specification conditioning on MKT-RF.

\textbf{OOS evidence: regime-redistributed, not independently replicated.}
Frozen OOS testing (2013--2024) detects an Elevated-regime signal
($F$-$p = 0.003$) that reflects post-GFC regime redistribution---the
frozen classifier reclassifies formerly Normal observations as Elevated---rather
than independent replication in the original Normal regime.
This result does not survive 30-pair Bonferroni correction and is sensitive to
prevalence (bootstrap $p = 0.153$), bandwidth ($p$ crosses 0.05), and
$K$ ($K = 2, 4$: null). By contrast, MOM$\to$SMB---the top-ranked OOS
pair---achieves near-perfect frozen OOS replication in the Normal regime
($F = 130.6$, $p < 10^{-28}$; in-sample $F = 130.7$), demonstrating that the
protocol can produce genuine OOS confirmation for sufficiently strong signals.

\textbf{Information-theoretic asymmetry with mechanistic explanation.}
Transfer entropy reveals that the reverse SMB$\to$HML channel carries
stronger nonlinear information than the linear forward channel
($z = 5.37$ vs.\ $z = 2.45$ in Normal), while both channels collapse
in Crisis ($z < 1.3$). Quantile Granger regression identifies the
mechanism: the reverse channel operates through \emph{tail dependence}
(upper-tail coefficient $8\times$ larger than median, Wald $p = 0.001$),
while the forward channel is homogeneous across quantiles (Wald $p = 0.906$).
This combined diagnostic---linear Granger for the forward channel,
quantile regression for the tail mechanism, transfer entropy for the
aggregate nonlinear signal---demonstrates that multiple diagnostic layers
are needed to fully map cross-factor information flow.
Importantly, this tail-dependence mechanism is \emph{pair-specific}:
applying quantile Granger to the top regime-heterogeneous pairs
(RMW$\to$SMB, rank~1, Wald $p = 0.869$; MKT$\to$SMB, $p = 0.527$;
SMB$\to$MKT, $p = 0.097$) reveals strictly linear within-regime dynamics.
Regime heterogeneity and quantile heterogeneity are thus distinct phenomena;
the nonlinear tail channel is specific to SMB$\to$HML, not a generic
feature of regime-heterogeneous factor pairs.

\textbf{Diagnostic protocol for computational finance.}
The regime-conditional analysis protocol developed here---multi-seed
HMM discovery, local-optima taxonomy, complexity characterization,
and information-theoretic diagnostics---assembles known techniques
into a reusable workflow applicable beyond HML--SMB to any set of
financial time series where latent-state structure may govern
predictive relationships. The individual methods are standard;
the diagnostic insight is that predictive models (OLS/RF/MLP/LSTM) and
information-theoretic measures (transfer entropy) can yield
qualitatively different diagnostic pictures within the same regime,
and both are needed to fully characterize cross-series dynamics.

\textbf{International replication: structural decay is global.}
Applying the full frozen OOS protocol to four non-US Fama-French datasets,
we find structural breaks in all four regions (Quandt-Andrews sup-$F$ exceeds
critical values everywhere). Two of four regions---Asia-Pacific ex Japan
(Crisis OOS $F = 39.39$, $p < 0.0001$) and Developed ex US
(Crisis OOS $F = 15.85$, $p = 0.0001$)---produce strong frozen OOS effects,
while Europe and Japan show in-sample significance but OOS nulls.
The heterogeneous replication pattern---with region-specific break dates
(2003--2014)---is itself consistent with the structural decay thesis:
cross-factor relationships erode at market-specific rather than universal dates.

\textbf{External validation eliminates circularity concerns.}
Replacing HMM-derived regimes with VIX-tercile regimes (entirely external
to factor returns), the structural break replicates: pre-2008 VIX-Normal
$p < 0.0001$, post-2008 $p = 0.714$. The finding is not an artifact
of regime-identification circularity.

\textbf{Implications.} Factor-timing models assuming regime-invariant cross-factor
relationships may misspecify dynamics during structural transitions.
Future work should pursue:
(1)~neural Granger methods~\cite{tank2022neural} for systematic
nonlinear analysis across factor networks;
(2)~end-to-end differentiable regime-conditional causal discovery
that jointly learns regimes and directed relationships;
(3)~13F holdings-based verification of the deleveraging mechanism;
(4)~\emph{pre-registered prospective validation}: applying the
frozen diagnostic protocol to a newly released international
factor dataset (e.g., emerging markets) with pair selection
and regime specification committed before data access,
providing the confirmatory OOS evidence that this exploratory
study cannot claim.

\section{Hypothesized Economic Mechanism}
\label{app:mechanism}

We offer the following as a \emph{hypothesis}, not a verified mechanism.
In Normal regimes, cross-factor inventory rebalancing may transmit value
shocks into size returns with a one-day delay. In stress periods, FF25
overlap is consistent with a deleveraging channel ($\rho_s = 0.35$,
permutation $p = 0.046$; Appendix~\ref{app:overlap}). That different
regimes may require different mechanisms (inventory vs.\ deleveraging)
is a limitation. Alternative explanations---common drivers (e.g., funding
liquidity), information propagation lags, or $K = 3$ specification
artifacts---cannot be ruled out~\cite{greenwood2011stock}.

\section{Portfolio Overlap Evidence}
\label{app:overlap}

The deleveraging hypothesis predicts that the HML$\to$SMB Granger link should
be strongest for portfolios that load on \emph{both} factors simultaneously.
The Small\-/HighBM portfolio sits in both the SMB long leg (small stocks) and
the HML long leg (high book-to-market), making it the primary candidate
transmission channel.

We test this prediction using the Fama-French 25 Size$\times$BM portfolios
(daily, 1990--2024). For each of the 25 portfolios, we run a Granger test
of HML$\to$Portfolio at lag 1 (BIC-selected in Table~\ref{tab:main}) within
the Crisis regime.

\textbf{Spatial gradient test (exploratory).} We define an ``overlap score'' for each
portfolio as $(4 - \text{size quintile}) + \text{BM quintile}$, ranging from
0 (Big/LowBM, no overlap) to 8 (Small/HighBM, maximal overlap). The
confirmatory test is a Spearman rank correlation between overlap score and
$-\log_{10}(p)$ of the Granger test across 25 portfolios, with significance
assessed by spatial permutation (10{,}000 shuffles of grid assignments;
time series are never touched).

\textbf{Result:} $\rho_s = 0.35$, permutation $p = 0.046$
(Figure~\ref{fig:ff25}). Granger significance concentrates in small-cap
portfolios: all five Small quintile portfolios are nominally significant
($p < 0.01$), with three surviving Bonferroni correction ($\alpha/25 = 0.002$):
S/L ($p = 2.5 \times 10^{-6}$, $\Delta R^2 = 1.47\%$),
S/3 ($p = 0.0014$), and S/H ($p \approx 0$, $\Delta R^2 = 1.79\%$).
One Big-quintile portfolio is nominally significant ($p < 0.01$), but none
survive Bonferroni. This spatial pattern is inconsistent
with a uniform factor-level effect and supports the overlap channel.

\textbf{Overlap fraction.} At lag 15,
\[
\frac{\Delta R^2(\text{HML}\to\text{S/H})}{\Delta R^2(\text{HML}\to\text{SMB})}=0.392
\]
Small\-/HighBM alone accounts for 39\% of the HML$\to$SMB incremental $R^2$.

\section{Markov-Switching VAR Comparison}

A single-stage MS-VAR~\cite{krolzig1997markov} ($K = 3$, 9 lags, switching variance)

parameters. The HML--SMB link exists within a principled single-stage framework
but its magnitude is too small to justify the model complexity.

\section{Trading and Risk Monitoring}
\label{app:trading}
\label{app:var}

\textbf{Trading backtest.}
A proof-of-concept strategy---long SMB when 9-day cumulative HML is positive
during Crisis, short otherwise---yields Sharpe $= -0.07$ (vs.\ buy-and-hold
$+0.06$) over 1995--2024. This intentionally simple rule (no optimization,
no transaction costs) confirms that \emph{statistical predictability}
$\neq$ \emph{economic predictability}; it does not rule out
profitability under more sophisticated implementations.

\textbf{VaR comparison: GARCH(1,1) vs.\ regime-conditional.}
We formally compare three 1\% VaR models on OOS data (2013--2024, $n = 3{,}020$):
(1)~GARCH(1,1): violation rate 1.48\% (target 1\%), Christoffersen
conditional coverage $p = 0.066$ (\emph{passes} at 5\%);
(2)~Regime-conditional (base): violation rate 3.31\%,
Christoffersen $p < 0.001$ (\emph{fails});
(3)~Regime-conditional with Granger adjustment: identical to base
(0 adjustment days triggered), violation rate 3.31\%.
GARCH(1,1) is clearly superior for point VaR coverage, with deviation
of only $+0.48$pp vs.\ $+2.31$pp for regime-conditional models.
However, the regime-conditional model achieves a higher tail hit rate
(56.3\% vs.\ 44.8\%), detecting more actual tail events at the cost of
substantially more false alarms.
The Granger adjustment mechanism (widening VaR when regime-conditional
Granger signals elevated cross-factor risk) failed to trigger during
the OOS period, indicating that the cross-factor predictive signal
does not translate into improved VaR timing under this implementation.
This confirms that the paper's contribution is \emph{diagnostic}---identifying
when cross-factor relationships shift---rather than providing a
deployable risk model that outperforms standard GARCH.

\section{Extended Robustness Analyses}
\label{app:robustness}

\textbf{Subsample stability.} Splitting at 2008: Crisis HML$\to$SMB
pre-2008 ($n = 659$, $p = 0.335$) and post-2008 ($n = 322$, $p = 0.599$)
are both non-significant, so we avoid claims of subsample-confirmed Crisis effects.

\textbf{Alternative regime methods.} Threshold-based volatility regimes
(realized volatility $>$ 90th percentile = Crisis) also detect the pattern
in sensitivity checks, but we treat this as exploratory given the primary-fit
null in Crisis at lag 1.

\textbf{Practitioner baselines (exploratory).} Rolling 250-day Granger tests
find HML$\to$SMB significant in 36.2\% of windows vs.\ 9.6\% reverse,
with concentration in Normal (47.1\%). FEVD spillover is regime-insensitive,
missing the Normal/Crisis directionality shift.
Weekly returns yield non-significant Crisis ($p = 0.114$, $n = 221$).

\textbf{Regime transitions (exploratory).} Analyzing 60-day windows around Normal$\to$Crisis
transitions, the HML$\to$SMB relationship strengthens upon crisis entry
(median $p$: $0.058 \to 0.021$), suggesting discrete intensification of the
predictive link.

\textbf{ML diagnostic protocol details} (supporting Section~\ref{sec:ml_validation}).
We apply four model classes---Linear (OLS), RF (100 trees), MLP (64-32),
LSTM (32 hidden, 9-step)---to characterize
the \emph{complexity boundary} of the predictive relationship.
Each predicts SMB from its own lags (restricted) vs.\ both SMB and
HML lags (unrestricted), with permutation testing
(200 shuffles for Linear/RF/MLP, 100 for LSTM; at 200 permutations,
$p$-value SE $\approx 0.007$ at $p = 0.01$) and expanding-window CV.

Table~\ref{tab:neural} and Figure~\ref{fig:complexity} present the results.

($p > 0.16$ throughout); however, under seed~42, MLP is marginally significant
($p = 0.015$), indicating fit-dependence.\footnote{The LSTM was estimated under
a separate HMM initialization; $p$-values are approximate due to the 100-permutation limit.}
This complexity characterization supports a conservative conclusion:
incremental predictive content in the HML$\to$SMB direction is captured by
linear terms, though TE reveals residual nonlinear structure in the reverse
direction (Table~\ref{tab:te}).

\section{Local Optima Taxonomy and Boundary Exclusion Details}
\label{app:optima}

This section provides technical details supplementing the main-text discussion in Section~\ref{sec:local_optima},
which presents the taxonomy and its economic implications in condensed form.

\textbf{Local optima taxonomy.}
HMM estimation via EM is sensitive to initialization. Our 50-seed multistart
reveals 7 distinct local optima clusters:
Cluster~1 (3 seeds, best BIC~$= 75{,}587$, 0\% GFC detection, Crisis $p = 0.88$);
Cluster~2 (15 seeds, BIC~$= 75{,}625$, 0\% GFC, Crisis $p < 0.001$);
Cluster~3 (8 seeds, BIC~$= 75{,}660$, 0\% GFC, Crisis $p = 0.001$);
Cluster~4 (8 seeds, BIC~$= 75{,}726$, 0\% GFC, Crisis $p < 0.001$);
Cluster~5 (7 seeds, BIC~$= 75{,}805$, 90\% GFC, Crisis $p = 0.018$);
Cluster~6 (3 seeds, BIC~$= 75{,}906$, 100\% GFC, Crisis $p = 0.17$);
Cluster~7 (6 seeds, BIC~$= 76{,}137$, 92\% GFC, Crisis $p = 0.28$).
Only clusters with $\geq$50\% GFC detection (5--7) are economically valid for
crisis-regime inference; among these, Cluster~5 has the best fit.
The highest log-likelihood cluster (seeds 28, 20, 6; BIC~$= 75{,}587$)
assigns 0\% of 2008 crisis days to the Crisis regime ($p = 0.88$),
while a lower-likelihood cluster (seeds 21, 9, 0, 29, 46, 36, 11;
BIC~$= 75{,}805$, $\Delta\text{BIC} = 218$) assigns 90\% of 2008 to Crisis
($p = 0.018$). Readers who prefer pure BIC selection should interpret the
Crisis-regime Granger results as seed-dependent; the Elevated-regime OOS results
(primary seed 28) are robust across all 7 clusters ($p < 0.05$ in 7/7),
though this robustness is conditional on the full OOS Elevated sample;
bootstrap prevalence reweighting yields median $p = 0.153$
(Section~\ref{sec:frozen_oos}).

\textbf{Boundary exclusion.}
In-sample: 0.67\% (59/8{,}817) excluded; OOS: 7.4\% (224/3{,}020).
Median inter-regime gap: 3 calendar days; exclusion rates are not
systematically biased toward any regime. This conservative approach
may underestimate transition effects but ensures clean within-regime estimation.
