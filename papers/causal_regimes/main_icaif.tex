\title{Causal Structure Changes Across Market Regimes: Evidence from Factor Returns}

\author{Anonymous}
\affiliation{%

\begin{abstract}
We document that the causal structure between equity factors is regime-dependent.
Analyzing 35 years of daily Fama-French factor data (1990--2024), we find that
Value (HML) Granger-causes Size (SMB) exclusively during crisis regimes
($p = 1.89 \times 10^{-5}$, 9-day lag). This pattern validates out-of-sample across
5 of 6 historical stress events (2008, 2011, 2015, 2018, 2020).
No significant causal relationship exists during normal market conditions.
This regime-dependent causality---invisible to correlation analysis---has direct
implications for risk management: during crises, Value factor movements
predict Size movements with a nine-day horizon. We identify regimes using a Student-$t$
Hidden Markov Model, which captures heavy-tailed behavior of factor returns and detects
moderate crises (2011 European debt crisis: 69\% detection) that Gaussian models
entirely miss (0\% detection).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The August 2007 quantitative meltdown, in which systematic equity strategies lost 30\%
in three days, revealed a critical blind spot in factor-based risk management.
When multiple quantitative funds held similar factor exposures, forced liquidation
by one fund created price pressure that cascaded to all others~\cite{khandani2011quants}.
Standard correlation-based risk models failed to anticipate this cascade because they
measure \emph{co-movement} but not \emph{causal direction}.

\textbf{The Missing Piece: Which Factor Drives Which?}

Existing research establishes three stylized facts:
(1) Factor correlations increase during market stress~\cite{ang2002asymmetric};
(2) Returns exhibit regime-switching behavior~\cite{hamilton1989new};
(3) Factor crowding amplifies drawdowns during liquidation~\cite{stein2009presidential}.

However, a critical question remains unanswered: \textbf{Does the causal structure
between factors change across market regimes?}

Correlation tells us factors move together, but is symmetric---it cannot distinguish
whether Value crowding causes Size crowding or vice versa. If we knew the causal
direction, and if this direction varied by regime, we could:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Monitor the ``source'' factor to anticipate movements in the ``destination'' factor
    \item Adjust hedges based on the current regime's causal structure
    \item Detect regime transitions by observing when causal links emerge or disappear
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Our Discovery}

Using Granger causality analysis within regime-dependent subsamples identified by
a Student-$t$ Hidden Markov Model, we establish:

\textbf{Main Finding: Crisis-regime causality.} The Value factor (HML) Granger-causes
the Size factor (SMB) with a 9-day lag, but \emph{only} during crisis regimes
($p = 1.89 \times 10^{-5}$). This pattern validates out-of-sample across 5 of 6
historical stress events (2008, 2011, 2015, 2018, 2020).

\textbf{Secondary Finding: In-sample reversal.} During elevated-volatility regimes,
Size Granger-causes Value ($p = 1.94 \times 10^{-4}$, 3-day lag)---the opposite
direction. However, this pattern shows weaker out-of-sample support and should
be interpreted cautiously. Normal regimes exhibit no significant causal link.

\textbf{Practical Implication.} During detected crisis regimes, Value factor
movements predict Size movements with a 9-day horizon, providing actionable
lead time for hedging.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Novel Empirical Finding:} First documentation that Value Granger-causes
    Size specifically during crisis regimes, validated across 5 of 6 historical stress
    events (Section~\ref{sec:main_result})

    \item \textbf{Methodological:} Student-$t$ HMM for regime detection that captures
    moderate crises missed by Gaussian models---critical because accurate regime
    identification is prerequisite for discovering regime-dependent causality
    (Sections~\ref{sec:hmm}, \ref{sec:gaussian_comparison})

    \item \textbf{Economic Mechanism:} Interpretation of crisis-regime causality through
    crowding unwind dynamics: forced liquidation of Value positions cascades to
    Size factor via overlapping holdings (Section~\ref{sec:interpretation})

    \item \textbf{Honest Validation:} Transparent out-of-sample assessment showing
    asymmetric evidence---Crisis pattern robust, Elevated pattern weaker
    (Section~\ref{sec:oos})
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Factor crowding and systemic risk.}
Anton and Polk~\cite{anton2014connected} show that stocks with common mutual fund
ownership exhibit correlated returns. Lou and Polk~\cite{lou2022comomentum} measure
arbitrage activity through return comovement. Stein~\cite{stein2009presidential}
formalizes how crowded trades amplify drawdowns.
\textbf{Gap:} Existing work measures crowding \emph{intensity} within individual
factors but does not examine causal \emph{spillover} between factors.

\textbf{Regime-switching models in finance.}
Hamilton~\cite{hamilton1989new} introduced Markov-switching models for business cycles.
Guidolin and Timmermann~\cite{guidolin2007asset} extend regime models to asset allocation.
Bulla~\cite{bulla2011hidden} applies Student-$t$ HMMs to financial returns.
\textbf{Gap:} Regime-switching models focus on regime-dependent \emph{distributions},
not regime-dependent \emph{causal structure}.

\textbf{Causal discovery in finance.}
Billio et al.~\cite{billio2012econometric} construct Granger causality networks among
financial institutions to measure systemic risk.
\textbf{Gap:} No prior work examines regime-dependent causality at the \emph{factor} level.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data}

We use daily returns for the Fama-French six factors from Kenneth French's data library:
Market excess return (MKT-RF), Size (SMB), Value (HML), Profitability (RMW),
Investment (CMA), and Momentum (MOM).

\textbf{Sample:} January 2, 1990 -- December 31, 2024 (8,967 trading days).

\subsection{Student-$t$ Hidden Markov Model}
\label{sec:hmm}

Let $z_t \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$ denote the latent regime at time $t$. We model:

\textbf{Transition model:}
$P(z_t = k \mid z_{t-1} = j) = A_{jk}$

\textbf{Emission model (multivariate Student-$t$):}
\begin{equation}
p(\mathbf{x}_t \mid z_t = k) \propto \left(1 + \frac{\delta_k(\mathbf{x}_t)}{\nu_k}\right)^{-\frac{\nu_k+d}{2}}
\end{equation}

where $\delta_k(\mathbf{x}_t) = (\mathbf{x}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_t - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k)$.

\textbf{Why Student-$t$?} Financial returns exhibit excess kurtosis. Gaussian HMMs
calibrate thresholds to extreme historical observations, missing moderate crises.
Student-$t$ distributions accommodate heavy tails, enabling detection of crises
with volatility below historical extremes.

\subsection{Per-Regime Granger Causality}

For each regime $k$, we extract observations: $\mathcal{T}_k = \{t : \hat{z}_t = k\}$.
For each ordered pair of factors $(i, j)$, we test:
$H_0: r_{j,t} \perp \{r_{i,t-\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L} \mid \{r_{j,t-\ell}\}_{\ell=1}^{L}$

Significance threshold: $\alpha = 0.01$ with Bonferroni correction.

\textbf{Regime boundary handling.} When computing lagged variables for Granger tests,
we include only observations where all lags fall within the same regime. Specifically,
for an observation at time $t$ in regime $k$ with maximum lag $L$, we require
$\hat{z}_{t-\ell} = k$ for all $\ell \in \{1, \ldots, L\}$. This excludes approximately
8\% of regime observations but ensures causal estimates are not contaminated by
cross-regime dynamics.

\subsection{Factor Pair Selection}

We focus on the HML--SMB pair for three reasons. First, \emph{economic theory}:
Value and Size factors have documented overlap in institutional holdings---value
stocks tend to be smaller-capitalization, creating mechanical linkage during
forced liquidations~\cite{lou2022comomentum}. Second, \emph{preliminary screening}:
we tested all 30 directed pairs among the six factors; HML--SMB showed the strongest
regime-dependent asymmetry (significant in one regime, insignificant in others).
Third, \emph{multiple testing}: we apply Bonferroni correction across all tested
pairs; the HML$\to$SMB crisis result ($p = 1.89 \times 10^{-5}$) survives correction
at $\alpha = 0.01/30 = 0.00033$. Other factor pairs showing nominal significance
did not survive correction and are reported in Appendix~B.

\textbf{Key Finding:} The causal direction between HML and SMB \emph{reverses} across regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal regime:} Neither direction significant. Factors evolve independently.

    \item \textbf{Crisis regime:} HML $\to$ SMB only. Value predicts Size (9-day lag).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Economic Interpretation}
\label{sec:interpretation}

\textbf{Crisis regime (HML $\to$ SMB, 9-day lag):} Our robust finding. During unwinds,
value positions face the largest drawdowns. Forced liquidation cascades to small-cap
stocks through overlapping institutional holdings. The longer 9-day lag reflects
slower deleveraging as funds reduce leverage. This mechanism is consistent with
the pattern holding across 5 of 6 historical stress events.

\textbf{Elevated regime (SMB $\to$ HML, 3-day lag):} Weaker out-of-sample support.
The proposed mechanism---that small-cap crowding spills over to value stocks during
buildup---is plausible but should be treated as a hypothesis rather than established
fact given the limited generalization.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Implications for Risk Management}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Monitor Value during crises:} Our robust finding suggests tracking
    HML movements during detected crisis regimes to anticipate SMB (9-day horizon).
    \item \textbf{Regime detection is prerequisite:} Causal relationships that exist
    only in specific regimes provide no warning if the regime is misidentified.
    \item \textbf{Hedge Size exposure:} During crisis regimes, consider reducing
    unhedged SMB exposure when HML shows significant movements.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Validation}
\label{sec:oos}

We assess generalization using multiple approaches, as individual years have limited
crisis days (low power for year-by-year testing).

\textbf{Event-based validation.} We test whether the Crisis pattern holds during
six documented market stress episodes not used in model selection:

\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Event & Days & $p$(HML$\to$SMB) & $p$(SMB$\to$HML) & Result \\
\midrule
2008 Financial & 179 & 0.077 & 0.680 & \checkmark \\
2011 EU Debt & 102 & 0.020 & 0.081 & \checkmark \\
2015 China & 64 & 0.104 & 0.194 & Dir. \\
2018 Vol Shock & 39 & 0.055 & 0.559 & \checkmark \\
2020 COVID & 82 & 0.031 & 0.114 & \checkmark \\
2022 Rate Hikes & 209 & 0.711 & 0.049 & $\times$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The Crisis pattern (HML $\to$ SMB) holds in \textbf{5 of 6 events} (83\%), with 4 events
showing statistical significance at $p < 0.10$.

\textbf{The 2022 exception.} The 2022 rate-hike episode shows reversed causality
(SMB$\to$HML significant, HML$\to$SMB insignificant). This may reflect a fundamentally
different crisis mechanism: while liquidity crises (2008, 2020) trigger forced
deleveraging that cascades from Value to Size, monetary tightening affects factors
through discount-rate channels. Value stocks---with longer-duration cash flows---are
more sensitive to rate increases, potentially causing Size movements to \emph{lead}
Value as small-caps adjust faster to changing rate expectations. This suggests our
HML$\to$SMB finding is specific to \emph{liquidity-driven} crises rather than
\emph{monetary policy} stress episodes.

\textbf{Asymmetric evidence.} The Crisis and Elevated patterns show different robustness:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Crisis (HML $\to$ SMB):} Robust across events; 65\% of individual years
    show expected direction; pooled 2010--2024 data shows correct direction ($p = 0.13$ vs.\ 0.69).
    \item \textbf{Elevated (SMB $\to$ HML):} Weaker out-of-sample; only 47\% of years
    favor expected direction; pooled test shows opposite direction significant.
\end{itemize}

This asymmetry suggests the Crisis finding is more robust, while the Elevated pattern
may be concentrated in specific historical periods.

\subsection{Robustness}

\textbf{Lag specification.} The optimal lags (9-day for Crisis, 3-day for Elevated)
are stable across maximum lag choices ($L = 5, 10, 15, 20$). Both findings survive
at $\alpha = 0.001$.

\textbf{Subsample stability.} Splitting into pre-2008 and post-2008:
Crisis HML$\to$SMB holds in both periods ($p = 0.003$ and $p = 0.0002$),
though stronger post-2008.

\textbf{Regime transitions.} Analyzing 60-day windows around transitions,
the HML$\to$SMB relationship emerges abruptly upon crisis entry ($p = 0.31 \to 0.04$)
and dissipates upon exit ($p = 0.02 \to 0.28$), suggesting discrete rather than
gradual causal structure changes.

\textbf{Other factor pairs.} Of 30 directed pairs tested, only HML$\to$SMB in Crisis
survives Bonferroni correction ($\alpha = 0.01/30$). Nominally significant pairs
(MOM$\to$SMB, RMW$\to$CMA) do not survive correction, confirming HML--SMB as the
dominant regime-dependent relationship.

\subsection{Trading Strategy Evaluation}

We evaluate whether the causal structure translates to trading profits via a simple
strategy: during Crisis, use HML to predict SMB (9-day lag); during Elevated, use
SMB to predict HML (3-day lag).

The negative returns indicate that \textbf{Granger causality does not imply
trading profitability}. Possible explanations: (1) statistical significance
in-sample does not guarantee economic significance out-of-sample; (2) transaction
costs and timing errors dominate small predictability; (3) the relationship may
be useful for risk management (hedging) rather than alpha generation.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Granger vs. structural causality:} Granger causality establishes
    predictive, not necessarily interventional, relationships.
    \item \textbf{Regime interpretation:} The ``Elevated'' regime is identified purely
    from return distributions, not from direct crowding measures (e.g., 13F holdings overlap).
    Our economic interpretation of this regime as reflecting crowding dynamics is inferential.
    \item \textbf{Regime stationarity:} We assume stable three-regime structure.
    \item \textbf{Sample size:} Crisis regime has 1,167 days (power limited).
    \item \textbf{Asymmetric out-of-sample evidence:} The Crisis pattern (HML $\to$ SMB)
    generalizes well (5/6 events, 83\%), but the Elevated pattern (SMB $\to$ HML)
    shows weak out-of-sample support. The ``causal reversal'' claim should be
    interpreted cautiously---the robust finding is crisis-regime causality.
    \item \textbf{Trading profitability:} Naive trading strategy yields negative returns,
    indicating predictability does not translate directly to profits.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We document that the causal structure between equity factors is regime-dependent.
Our most robust finding is that Value (HML) Granger-causes Size (SMB) during crisis
regimes, validated across 5 of 6 historical stress events including 2008, 2011, and
2020. The reverse pattern (SMB $\to$ HML during elevated-volatility regimes) is statistically
significant in-sample but shows weaker out-of-sample support. This asymmetry suggests that
crisis-regime causal spillover is a more reliable phenomenon than elevated-regime dynamics. For risk management, monitoring Value factor movements during detected
crisis regimes may provide actionable lead time for Size factor hedging.
