\begin{abstract}
Conformal prediction provides distribution-free coverage guarantees, but these degrade under distribution shift---and practitioners lack tools to anticipate \textit{which} deployed models will fail before deployment.
We propose SHAP concentration---the fraction of feature importance concentrated
in the top feature---as a \textbf{pre-deployment diagnostic} for conformal
prediction vulnerability. Using COVID-19 as a case study across 8
supply chain tasks experiencing identical temporal shift yet coverage drops ranging from 0.1\% to 77\% (all paired $p \leq 0.005$, 50
seeds), we show that, for gradient-boosted models, SHAP concentration is strongly associated with failure severity: $\rho = 0.853$, $p < 0.001$ across 16 multiclass tasks in 9 domains (bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.50, 0.96]$; Kendall $\tau = 0.667$, $p < 0.001$). Standard shift detectors (MMD, C2ST) detect shift for all tasks uniformly but do not distinguish catastrophic from robust outcomes ($\rho \leq 0.19$, all $p > 0.6$); among evaluated pre-deployment diagnostics, SHAP concentration shows the strongest association with failure severity. External validation across 9 held-out domains shows partial transfer with asymmetric evidence: Covertype ($C=49.8\%$, 82~pp drop) is deterministic across 10 seeds, most low-concentration datasets are deterministically robust, Shuttle is near-deterministic (9/10), and KDDCup99/Stack Overflow are boundary cases. We prove a formal score-inflation theorem under explicit assumptions showing that APS conformity-score bounds worsen monotonically with concentration, verified on all applicable tasks. A decision framework operationalizes the diagnostic as an exploratory rule: compute SHAP concentration pre-deployment; consider quarterly retraining if vulnerable (+19~pp recovery, $p = 0.04$); defer routine retraining for lower-risk tasks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Conformal prediction provides distribution-free coverage guarantees under the assumption of exchangeability~\citep{vovk2005algorithmic}. However, real-world deployments face distribution shifts that violate this assumption. While prior work characterizes \textit{how} conformal prediction degrades under shift~\citep{tibshirani2019conformal, barber2023conformal}, a critical gap remains: \textbf{Can we identify which deployed models will fail before observing test data?}

We propose \textbf{SHAP concentration}---the fraction of total feature importance concentrated in the top feature---as a pre-deployment diagnostic for conformal prediction vulnerability. Using a supply chain benchmark with temporal splits aligned to COVID-19 (February--July 2020), we conduct an observational case study where 8 classification tasks experience identical temporal shift yet exhibit coverage drops ranging from negligible ($<$1\%) to catastrophic ($>$70\%).

Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Pre-deployment diagnostic}: SHAP concentration, computed solely on validation data, correlates with catastrophic conformal failure under severe shift. Across 16 multiclass tasks in 9 domains: $\rho = 0.853$, $p < 0.001$ (Kendall $\tau = 0.667$, $p < 0.001$; bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.50, 0.96]$). Within supply chain alone: $\rho = 0.833$, $p = 0.010$ ($n=8$). SHAP concentration achieves stronger correlation than alternative pre-deployment diagnostics---native feature importance ($\rho = 0.667$, $p = 0.071$) and ensemble disagreement ($\rho = 0.452$, $p = 0.260$)---though the difference is not formally testable at $n=8$. Binary APS exhibits a structural ceiling effect; concentration is diagnostic specifically for multiclass settings.

    \item \textbf{Formal theory}: Theorem~\ref{thm:score_inflation} establishes that, under an additive feature-decomposition model, APS score and coverage bounds worsen monotonically with concentration parameter $C$ (Section~\ref{sec:theory}). The bound is verified on all 5 applicable tasks. We confirm empirically that test conformity scores stochastically dominate calibration scores for catastrophic tasks (KS $= 0.68$--$0.96$, all $p < 10^{-10}$; Figure~\ref{fig:cdfs}). Catastrophic tasks show \textit{decreasing} prediction entropy---the opposite of standard intuition---because the model misclassifies confidently rather than randomly.

    \item \textbf{Quantification with statistical rigor}: Coverage drops across 8 tasks range from 0.1\% to 77.1\%, all with paired $p \leq 0.005$ (Wilcoxon, 50 seeds). The correlation magnitude is stable under leave-one-out analysis ($\rho \in [0.75, 0.96]$), though significance is not maintained for 2 of 8 jackknife samples due to reduced power at $n=7$.

    \item \textbf{Operational framework (exploratory)}: Quarterly retraining can partially mitigate vulnerable task coverage loss by +19~pp ($p=0.04$); robust tasks (99.8\% coverage) show little intervention benefit in this setting. Under the at-risk criterion (drop $>$15~pp), the framework shows a precision--recall trade-off across 30--45\% thresholds (precision 0.71$\rightarrow$1.00; recall 0.83; Appendix~\ref{app:threshold}).

    \item \textbf{Binary ceiling effect}: Binary APS prediction sets ($\{0\}, \{1\}$, or $\{0,1\}$) are structurally protected against the coverage degradation that concentration detects. This boundary condition clarifies when the diagnostic applies (multiclass) and when it does not (binary).

    \item \textbf{External domain validation}: The SALT-derived 40\% threshold is partially transferable across 9 held-out domains without tuning. Ten-seed replication yields deterministic/near-deterministic behavior for 7/9 domains (6 deterministic, 1 near-deterministic): Forest Covertype is correctly flagged as catastrophic ($C = 49.8\%$, 10/10 seeds), while KDDCup99 ($C = 21.1\%$, drop $= 15.9\pm21.4$~pp) and Stack Overflow (near-binary ceiling) are boundary cases that require caveats; external catastrophic evidence is concentrated in Covertype.

    \item \textbf{Shift detection $\neq$ severity prediction}: Standard shift indicators (MMD, C2ST, PSI) show shift signal for \textit{all} 8 tasks uniformly (MMD $p<0.002$; C2ST $>$99.9\% accuracy; PSI $>0.002$), but cannot distinguish catastrophic from robust outcomes ($\rho \leq 0.22$, all $p > 0.6$). Among evaluated pre-deployment metrics, SHAP concentration best captures the model-specific dependence structure linked to failure severity.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Conformal Prediction.} \citet{vovk2005algorithmic} introduced conformal prediction with exchangeability guarantees. Recent work extends to classification~\citep{romano2020classification} and regression~\citep{romano2019conformalized}. \citet{angelopoulos2021gentle} provide a comprehensive tutorial.

\textbf{Conformal Prediction Under Shift.} \citet{tibshirani2019conformal} study covariate shift with known propensity scores. \citet{podkopaev2021distribution} develop distribution-free uncertainty quantification for classification under label shift. \citet{barber2023conformal} provide a comprehensive treatment beyond exchangeability, bounding coverage loss by the total variation distance between test and calibration score distributions. We complement these theoretical characterizations with an empirical diagnostic that is associated with this distributional divergence---and hence with which tasks fail---before deployment.

\textbf{Adaptive Methods.} \citet{gibbs2021adaptive} propose Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) for non-stationary settings, with extensions by \citet{zaffran2022adaptive} for time series and \citet{feldman2023achieving} for online risk control. \citet{angelopoulos2024conformal} generalize conformal prediction to broader risk measures. Our experiments show ACI recovers marginal coverage under severe shift but at a substantial cost in prediction set informativeness.

\textbf{Shift Detection.} WILDS~\citep{koh2021wilds} and Shifts~\citep{malinin2021shifts} provide benchmarks, while \citet{gulrajani2020search} show robust methods often fail under temporal shift. \citet{garg2022leveraging} use unlabeled test data to predict accuracy degradation---a related goal but requiring test-time observations. Kernel-based tests (MMD~\citep{gretton2012kernel}) and classifier two-sample tests (C2ST~\citep{lopez2017revisiting}) detect distributional differences but do not predict the \textit{severity} of downstream failure. We show empirically that MMD and C2ST detect shift for all tasks uniformly yet cannot distinguish catastrophic from robust conformal outcomes (Section~\ref{sec:shift_detection}). Our contribution is a \textit{pre-deployment} diagnostic using feature importance structure that predicts failure severity, not merely shift presence.

\textbf{Interpretability for Reliability.} Our work connects SHAP~\citep{lundberg2017unified} to model reliability assessment, extending the use of feature attribution from debugging~\citep{adebayo2018sanity} to prospective failure diagnosis.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Dataset and Temporal Splits}

We use the SALT (Supply chain ALlocaTion) dataset from RelBench~\citep{fey2023relbench}, a supply chain benchmark with temporal splits. All 8 classification tasks from the rel-salt benchmark are included; no tasks were excluded.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Train}: Before February 2020 (pre-COVID)
    \item \textbf{Validation}: February--July 2020 (COVID onset)
    \item \textbf{Test}: After July 2020 (COVID peak)
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Data separation protocol.} SHAP concentration and the 40\% threshold are computed exclusively on validation-time data. The test set is used only for final evaluation and never informs diagnostic thresholds. For threshold evaluation we use at-risk labels defined by observed test coverage drops (drop $>$15~pp); the threshold sensitivity analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:threshold} is therefore post-hoc validation, not prospective assessment.

\subsection{Conformal Prediction Setup}

We use Adaptive Prediction Sets (APS)~\citep{romano2020classification} with $\alpha = 0.1$ (90\% target coverage). We use the non-randomized variant (without the uniform tie-breaking variable $U$), which provides conservative rather than exact coverage. This choice does not affect the diagnostic analysis, as the same scoring rule is applied to both calibration and test sets.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Train LightGBM classifier with default hyperparameters
    \item Calibrate conformal predictor on 50\% of validation set
    \item Evaluate coverage on held-out validation and test sets
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ensemble Protocol}

We train 50 independent models with seeds 42--91. For each seed $s$: train model $M_s$, split validation 50/50 into calibration $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}^s$ and evaluation $\mathcal{D}_{\text{eval}}^s$, calibrate $\text{CP}_s$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}^s$, evaluate on both $\mathcal{D}_{\text{eval}}^s$ and test. We report mean, 95\% confidence intervals ($t$-distribution), and paired Wilcoxon tests (seed-level). This is \textit{not} ensemble prediction---each seed is an independent experimental trial providing paired comparisons.

\subsection{Feature Overlap Metric}

Feature temporal stability uses Jaccard similarity:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:jaccard}
J(x_j) = \frac{|A_{\text{train}} \cap A_{\text{test}}|}{|A_{\text{train}} \cup A_{\text{test}}|}
\end{equation}
where $A_{\text{train}}$ and $A_{\text{test}}$ are the sets of unique values for feature $x_j$ in train and test data. In the pre-deployment framework (Section~\ref{sec:framework}), we substitute validation data for test data as a proxy.

\subsection{SHAP Concentration Diagnostic}\label{sec:shap_method}

We define the SHAP concentration metric as:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:concentration}
C = \frac{\phi_1}{\sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_j$ is the mean absolute SHAP value of the $j$-th feature computed on validation data using TreeExplainer~\citep{lundberg2020local}, and $\phi_1$ is the importance of the top-ranked feature. $C$ is computed \textbf{before deployment} using only validation data.

\subsection{Why SHAP Concentration?}\label{sec:why_shap}

A natural question is whether simpler diagnostics suffice.

\textbf{Feature overlap (Jaccard) is insufficient.} Tasks relying on transaction IDs have Jaccard $\approx 0$ while tasks with entity-based features have Jaccard $> 0.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:overlap}), yet Jaccard alone cannot explain the full range of coverage drops.

\textbf{Post-hoc metrics require test data.} Prediction entropy changes and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) shifts can detect failures---but only \textit{after} observing test data (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}). Among the diagnostics we evaluate, SHAP concentration is available before deployment and shows the strongest severity association.

\textbf{Why not raw feature importance?} Raw importance values are not comparable across tasks (different scales, feature counts). Concentration normalizes to a $[0,1]$ scale, enabling cross-task comparison.

\textbf{Why top-1?} We compared top-1 concentration against alternatives: top-2, top-3, Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), and feature importance entropy. Top-1 concentration is the only variant among these five that reaches nominal significance with coverage drop ($\rho = 0.833$, $p = 0.010$); all alternatives are non-significant ($p > 0.10$). The discriminating power of top-1 arises because sales-office has high top-2/3 concentration (77--85\%) but near-zero coverage drop---the diagnostic signal is specifically whether a \textit{single} dominant feature drives predictions, not overall feature spread.

\section{Theory: Why Concentration Relates to Failure}\label{sec:theory}

We establish a mechanistic account for why SHAP concentration is associated with conformal failure under feature shift. The following theorem shows that when a model concentrates dependence on a single feature that shifts out of distribution, APS score and coverage bounds worsen monotonically with concentration parameter $C$, under explicit assumptions.

\textbf{Notation.} For a predicted distribution $\hat{p}(y \mid x) \in \Delta^{K-1}$, the APS conformity score $s(x,y^*)$ sums predicted probabilities from the most likely class down to and including $y^*$. Equivalently:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:aps_equiv}
s(x, y^*) \;=\; 1 \;-\!\!\!\!\sum_{\substack{y \neq y^* :\\ \hat{p}(y \mid x) < \hat{p}(y^* \mid x)}}\!\!\!\! \hat{p}(y \mid x),
\end{equation}
since $s$ includes all mass at or above the rank of $y^*$, leaving out only classes ranked strictly below. (The bound holds regardless of randomized tie-breaking conventions in APS implementations, since $\mathcal{B}$ uses strict inequality.)

\begin{theorem}[Score Inflation Under Concentrated Feature Shift]\label{thm:score_inflation}
Consider a $K$-class classifier ($K \geq 3$) with APS conformal predictor calibrated at level $\alpha$ with quantile $\hat{q}_\alpha$. Let $C \in [0,1]$ denote SHAP concentration on feature $x_1$. Assume:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(A\arabic*)},leftmargin=*,nosep]
\item \textbf{Additive decomposition.} $\hat{p}(y \mid x) = C \cdot g(y \mid x_1) + (1-C) \cdot h(y \mid x_{\setminus 1})$, where $g(\cdot \mid x_1), h(\cdot \mid x_{\setminus 1}) \in \Delta^{K-1}$.
\item \textbf{Concentrated misclassification.} Under shift, $g(y^* \mid x_1^{\mathrm{test}}) \leq \varepsilon$ for some $\varepsilon \in [0, 1/K)$.
\item \textbf{Residual exchangeability.} $(h(\cdot \mid x_{\setminus 1}^{\mathrm{test}}), y^*_{\mathrm{test}}) \stackrel{d}{=} (h(\cdot \mid x_{\setminus 1}^{\mathrm{cal}}), y^*_{\mathrm{cal}})$.
\end{enumerate}
Then:

\smallskip
\noindent\textup{(i)} \textbf{Pointwise score bound.} For each test instance $(x^{\mathrm{test}}, y^*)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:score_pointwise}
s(x^{\mathrm{test}}, y^*) \;\geq\; 1 - (K\!-\!1)\big[C\varepsilon + (1\!-\!C)\,h(y^*\!\mid\! x_{\setminus 1}^{\mathrm{test}})\big].
\end{equation}

\smallskip
\noindent\textup{(ii)} \textbf{Expected score inflation.} Let $\bar{h} := \mathbb{E}[h(y^*_{\mathrm{cal}} \mid x_{\setminus 1}^{\mathrm{cal}})]$ denote the expected true-class probability under the residual model. Then:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:score_bound}
\mathbb{E}[s_{\mathrm{test}}] \;\geq\; C(1\!-\!(K\!-\!1)\varepsilon) \;+\; (1\!-\!C)(1\!-\!(K\!-\!1)\bar{h}).
\end{equation}

\smallskip
\noindent\textup{(iii)} \textbf{Monotone vulnerability.} The bound in~\eqref{eq:score_bound} is strictly increasing in $C$ whenever $\varepsilon < \bar{h}$. A sufficient condition is $\bar{h} \geq 1/K$ (the residual model is at least uniform on the true class); together with \textup{(A2)} this implies $\varepsilon < \bar{h}$.

\smallskip
\noindent\textup{(iv)} \textbf{Coverage degradation bound.} Coverage satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cov_bound}
\mathbb{P}(y^* \!\in\! \mathcal{C}(x^{\mathrm{test}})) \;\leq\; \mathbb{P}\!\left(h(y^*_{\mathrm{cal}}) \geq T(C)\right),
\end{equation}
where $T(C) := \bigl[(1\!-\!\hat{q}_\alpha)/(K\!-\!1) - C\varepsilon\bigr]/(1\!-\!C)$ is strictly increasing in $C$ when $\varepsilon < (1\!-\!\hat{q}_\alpha)/(K\!-\!1)$ (satisfied at $\varepsilon = 0$). Hence the coverage upper bound is non-increasing in~$C$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Part (i).} Fix a test point $(x^{\mathrm{test}}, y^*)$ and write $\hat{p}(y) = \hat{p}(y \mid x^{\mathrm{test}})$. By~\eqref{eq:aps_equiv}, $s = 1 - L$ where $L := \sum_{y \in \mathcal{B}} \hat{p}(y)$ and $\mathcal{B} := \{y \neq y^* : \hat{p}(y) < \hat{p}(y^*)\}$. Since each term in $L$ is strictly less than $\hat{p}(y^*)$ and $|\mathcal{B}| \leq K-1$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:L_bound}
L \;\leq\; (K\!-\!1)\,\hat{p}(y^*).
\end{equation}
Under (A1), $\hat{p}(y^*) = C\,g(y^*\!\mid\! x_1^{\mathrm{test}}) + (1\!-\!C)\,h(y^*\!\mid\! x_{\setminus 1}^{\mathrm{test}})$. Applying (A2), $g(y^*) \leq \varepsilon$, so $\hat{p}(y^*) \leq C\varepsilon + (1\!-\!C)\,h(y^*)$. Substituting into~\eqref{eq:L_bound} and using $s = 1 - L$ establishes~\eqref{eq:score_pointwise}.

\textbf{Part (ii).} Taking expectations over $(x^{\mathrm{test}}, y^*)$ in~\eqref{eq:score_pointwise}:
$\mathbb{E}[s_{\mathrm{test}}] \geq 1 - (K\!-\!1)C\varepsilon - (K\!-\!1)(1\!-\!C)\,\mathbb{E}[h(y^*_{\mathrm{test}})]$.
Under (A3), $\mathbb{E}[h(y^*_{\mathrm{test}})] = \bar{h}$. Separating terms:
$1 - (K\!-\!1)C\varepsilon - (K\!-\!1)(1\!-\!C)\bar{h} = C[1\!-\!(K\!-\!1)\varepsilon] + (1\!-\!C)[1\!-\!(K\!-\!1)\bar{h}]$.

\textbf{Part (iii).} Write $B(C) = Ca + (1\!-\!C)b$ with $a = 1-(K\!-\!1)\varepsilon$ and $b = 1-(K\!-\!1)\bar{h}$. Then $B'(C) = a - b = (K\!-\!1)(\bar{h} - \varepsilon) > 0$ when $\varepsilon < \bar{h}$. Under (A2), $\varepsilon < 1/K$. For any classifier outperforming uniform on the true class, $\bar{h} \geq 1/K$, so $B'(C) > 0$.

\textbf{Part (iv).} From~\eqref{eq:score_pointwise}, $s_{\mathrm{test}} \leq \hat{q}_\alpha$ requires $(K\!-\!1)[C\varepsilon + (1\!-\!C)\,h(y^*)] \geq 1 - \hat{q}_\alpha$, i.e., $h(y^*) \geq T(C)$. Therefore $\mathbb{P}(s_{\mathrm{test}} \leq \hat{q}_\alpha) \leq \mathbb{P}(h(y^*_{\mathrm{test}}) \geq T(C))$, and (A3) gives~\eqref{eq:cov_bound}. Differentiating: $T'(C) = [(1\!-\!\hat{q}_\alpha)/(K\!-\!1) - \varepsilon]/(1\!-\!C)^2 > 0$ when $\varepsilon < (1\!-\!\hat{q}_\alpha)/(K\!-\!1)$.
\end{proof}

\textit{Discussion.} (A1) formalizes the SHAP decomposition: $C$ measures the model's dependence on $x_1$. Strictly, TreeSHAP decomposes in log-odds space while (A1) operates in probability space; the approximation is accurate when $C$ is high (the dominant feature's contribution overwhelms softmax nonlinearity) or low (both spaces are near-linear), which are precisely the regimes where the diagnostic is applied. (A2) captures concentrated misclassification: when $x_1$ takes values outside the training support, tree models extrapolate to the nearest in-distribution leaf, producing confident but wrong predictions ($\varepsilon \approx 0$). Catastrophic tasks are consistent with (A2) through strong rightward shifts in conformity scores (Table~\ref{tab:ks}), but we do not directly estimate $g(y^*\!\mid\! x_1^{\mathrm{test}})$. (A3) requires only that features unaffected by shift maintain their conditional relationship with labels; $\bar{h}$ is the expected true-class probability under the \textit{residual} model $h$ (not the full model), and the monotonicity result uses the explicit condition $\varepsilon < \bar{h}$ (or the sufficient condition $\bar{h} \geq 1/K$). The gap between the bound and observed scores reflects the $(K\!-\!1)$ factor in~\eqref{eq:L_bound}, which is tight only when $y^*$ is ranked last among classes with near-equal probabilities.

\textit{Bound verification.} Setting $\varepsilon = 0$ and $\bar{h} = 1/K$ (conservative: residual model no better than uniform), Theorem~\ref{thm:score_inflation}(ii) gives $\mathbb{E}[s_{\mathrm{test}}] \geq C + (1-C)/K$: s-shipcond ($C\!=\!0.78$, $K\!=\!45$) $\geq 0.785$ (observed $0.98$); s-payterms ($C\!=\!0.84$, $K\!=\!135$) $\geq 0.841$ (observed $0.92$); s-group ($C\!=\!0.99$, $K\!=\!462$) $\geq 0.990$ (observed $1.00$); i-plant ($C\!=\!0.80$, $K\!=\!35$) $\geq 0.806$ (observed $0.86$); s-incoterms ($C\!=\!0.81$, $K\!=\!13$) $\geq 0.825$ (observed $0.87$). The bound is satisfied for all five tasks where test scores shift rightward, with gaps of $0.05$--$0.20$ reflecting the $(K\!-\!1)$ factor and the conservative $\bar{h} = 1/K$ choice. Tasks where test scores shift \textit{leftward} (i-incoterms, i-shippoint) violate (A2): the model becomes more confident under shift, so the theorem does not apply. We verify the stochastic dominance prediction empirically below (Figure~\ref{fig:cdfs}, Table~\ref{tab:ks}).

\textbf{Connection to beyond-exchangeability theory.} \citet{barber2023conformal} show that conformal coverage degrades in proportion to the total variation distance between test and calibration score distributions. Our stochastic dominance finding (KS $= 0.68$--$0.96$ for catastrophic tasks) provides a direct empirical measure of this distributional divergence. SHAP concentration can be interpreted as a \textit{pre-deployment proxy} for score TV distance: high concentration implies the model's scores are sensitive to a single feature, so feature shift can inflate the TV bound; low concentration implies score distributions are less sensitive to any single feature's shift. Our results are consistent with Barber et al.'s framework: empirical coverage gaps scale with score-distribution divergence, and concentration tracks this tendency before test data is observed.

\textbf{Marginal vs.\ conditional coverage.} Our analysis evaluates \textit{marginal} coverage (averaged over all test instances). Conditional coverage---per-class or per-subgroup---may degrade even more severely when concentration drives misclassification toward specific classes. The conformity score stochastic dominance we observe is a marginal phenomenon; per-class analysis could reveal structured failure patterns. Extending the concentration diagnostic to conditional coverage is a natural direction for future work.

\textbf{Diversification protects.} When importance is distributed across $p$ features (low $C$), the effect of any single OOD feature is attenuated. Even if all features shift, the model's score distribution depends on a ``portfolio'' of features whose individual effects partially cancel. This is analogous to portfolio diversification: concentrated bets amplify risk; diversified exposure dampens it.

\textbf{Concentration is orthogonal to shift magnitude.} An important distinction: SHAP concentration measures \textit{vulnerability} (a model property), not the magnitude of realized score-distribution shift. Empirically, concentration and KS statistic are uncorrelated across the 8 SALT tasks ($\rho = 0.29$, $p = 0.49$). This orthogonality is expected: a low-concentration model can exhibit large KS statistics yet maintain coverage (the shift is absorbed across features), while a high-concentration model amplifies even moderate feature-level shift into score stochastic dominance. The diagnostic's value lies precisely in predicting \textit{which models will amplify shift into coverage failure}, not in measuring the shift itself---a task at which standard detectors (MMD, C2ST) already excel. (Note: sales-office has KS $= 0.994$---the highest in our study---yet is robust. This apparent paradox arises because both calibration and test score distributions are degenerate at $s \approx 1.0$; the large KS reflects numerical precision in near-identical distributions, not exchangeability violation.)

\textbf{Empirical verification.} Figure~\ref{fig:shap} is consistent with this argument: catastrophic tasks show concentrated importance explosion (4.5$\times$) in the top feature, while robust tasks redistribute importance across features (10--20$\times$ increases distributed). We directly verify the predicted stochastic dominance by computing per-instance APS conformity scores. For the most catastrophic task (sales-shipcond, 71.6\% drop), calibration and test score distributions differ dramatically (KS $= 0.956$, $p < 10^{-10}$): calibration scores spread across $[0.2, 1.0]$ (mean $= 0.54$) while test scores concentrate near $1.0$ (mean $= 0.98$), confirming test scores stochastically dominate calibration scores (Figure~\ref{fig:cdfs}). All three catastrophic tasks show this pattern: test score means exceed calibration means by 0.02--0.44. In contrast, the robust task sales-office shows both distributions compressed near $1.0$ (means $0.996$ vs.\ $1.000$). Full KS statistics for all 8 tasks in Appendix~\ref{app:ks}.

\subsection{SHAP Concentration Correlates with Failure}\label{sec:shap_results}

Among 8 tasks with severe feature turnover, SHAP concentration correlates with coverage degradation: Spearman $\rho = 0.833$, $p = 0.010$ (Table~\ref{tab:stratified_correlation}, Figure~\ref{fig:n11_correlation}). Bootstrap 95\% CI: $[0.29, 1.00]$ (10,000 resamples). The correlation magnitude is stable under leave-one-out analysis ($\rho \in [0.75, 0.96]$), though statistical significance is not maintained for 2 of 8 jackknife samples ($p = 0.052$) due to reduced power at $n=7$. SHAP concentration values are highly stable: bootstrap resampling (1,000 resamples of 10K validation samples) yields coefficient of variation below 1\% for all tasks, with 95\% CIs within $\pm$1 percentage point.

\textbf{Mechanism.} Figure~\ref{fig:shap} reveals the distinction. Catastrophic tasks: the dominant feature experiences a concentrated importance explosion (4.5$\times$ increase). Robust tasks: importance redistributes across features with ranking reshuffle (10--20$\times$ increases distributed). This matches the prediction of Theorem~\ref{thm:score_inflation}.

\textbf{False positives.} Two tasks with concentration $>$40\% remain robust: sales-office (secondary feature SALESORG has Jaccard $= 0.61$) and item-shippoint (high model variance). These motivate the protective-factor check in our framework (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).

\textbf{Task independence.} Intraclass correlation analysis confirms task identity explains the majority of seed-level variance in coverage drops (ICC $= 0.675$, 95\% CI $[0.47, 0.90]$; effective $n_{\text{eff}} = 11.7$). Per-task ICC(val,test) is negative for all 8 tasks, indicating paired tests are conservative. Details in Appendix~\ref{app:icc}.

\textbf{Cross-domain extension.} Extending to 16 multiclass tasks across 9 domains---including 8 external datasets spanning 7 types of natural distribution shift (Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain})---yields $\rho=0.853$, $p < 0.001$ (Kendall $\tau = 0.667$; bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.50, 0.96]$). The external datasets confirm the diagnostic in both directions: correctly flagging catastrophic failure (Covertype, 82~pp drop) and preserving robust behavior for most low-concentration datasets, with KDDCup99 as a seed-dependent intermediate regime.

\subsection{Diagnostic Comparison}\label{sec:baselines}

We compare SHAP concentration against two alternative pre-deployment diagnostics that require no test data (Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic_comparison}).

\textbf{Native feature importance concentration}: top-1 LightGBM gain divided by total gain (zero SHAP cost, single seed). Achieves $\rho = 0.667$ ($p = 0.071$)---directionally correct but not significant at $\alpha = 0.05$. The weaker discrimination arises because native gain conflates feature cardinality with predictive importance: sales-office shows 66.1\% native concentration (vs.\ 42.6\% SHAP) despite near-zero coverage drop.

\textbf{Ensemble disagreement}: standard deviation of validation coverage across 50 seeds (seed 42). Achieves $\rho = 0.452$ ($p = 0.260$). Disagreement captures model instability but not the feature-level mechanism driving conformal failure.

\textbf{Class cardinality as confound.} Within SALT, $\log(\text{num\_classes})$ alone correlates with coverage drop ($\rho = 0.743$, $p = 0.035$), and partial correlations are non-significant at $n=8$ ($\rho_{\text{partial}} = 0.629$ for concentration, $0.334$ for cardinality). KDDCup99 (11, $21.1\%$) is a seed-dependent intermediate case rather than a clean robust counterexample.

\subsection{Alternative Conformal Scoring}\label{sec:raps}

A natural question is whether alternative conformal scoring functions mitigate the failures we identify. We evaluate Regularized Adaptive Prediction Sets (RAPS)~\citep{angelopoulos2021uncertainty}, which adds a regularization penalty $\lambda(|C| - k_{\text{reg}})^+$ to encourage smaller prediction sets ($\lambda=0.01$, $k_{\text{reg}}=5$; 10 seeds for severe tasks).

For many-class tasks, RAPS substantially reduces drops through regularization: sales-group (462 classes) drops from $73.5\pm33.9\%$ (APS) to $10.4\pm1.3\%$ (RAPS, 10 seeds)---a dramatic recovery explained by RAPS capping set sizes well below the class count. Sales-payterms (135 classes) partially recovers: $79.9\pm29.4\%$ to $35.1\pm28.6\%$, though with high seed variance. However, the most concentrated task (sales-shipcond, 45 classes, $C=50.7\%$) shows \textit{no improvement}: APS drop $= 60.4\pm31.8\%$, RAPS drop $= 67.8\pm25.4\%$. For tasks with few classes ($\leq$35), RAPS produces identical coverage drops to APS (differences ${<}0.5$~pp). Full per-task comparison in Appendix~\ref{app:raps}.

The key insight: RAPS regularization reduces the \textit{effective score range} by penalizing set accumulation beyond $k_{\text{reg}}$ classes, which helps when many-class accumulation amplifies the exchangeability violation---each additional class contributes marginal probability mass that compounds the score inflation. For sales-group (462 classes), RAPS truncates this accumulation, dramatically reducing the drop. However, when single-feature dependence causes complete score stochastic dominance (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), the wrong class receives the dominant probability mass \textit{before} regularization activates ($k_{\text{reg}}=5$), so no scoring rule adjustment suffices. This explains the precise pattern: RAPS helps high-cardinality tasks (where class accumulation drives failure) but not high-concentration tasks (where single-feature dependence drives failure). The distinction reinforces the value of pre-deployment diagnostics: identifying the \textit{mechanism} of failure determines whether scoring rule alternatives can help.

\subsection{Comparison with Standard Shift Detection}\label{sec:shift_detection}

A natural objection is that standard distribution shift detectors (MMD, classifier two-sample tests) might serve as pre-deployment diagnostics. We evaluate three approaches on the 8 SALT tasks (Table~\ref{tab:shift_compare}).

\textbf{Methods.} (1)~Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) with RBF kernel and median heuristic bandwidth (500 permutations, $n=1000$ subsample); (2)~Classifier Two-Sample Test (C2ST) using LightGBM with 5-fold stratified CV; (3)~Population Stability Index (PSI) with 10 quantile bins.

\textbf{Result.} All three methods show shift signal for \textit{every} task. MMD provides statistical evidence of shift ($p < 0.002$ for all 8), C2ST shows near-perfect domain discrimination (accuracy $> 99.9\%$ for all 8), and PSI is consistently non-zero ($> 0.002$ for all 8). However, none can distinguish catastrophic from robust tasks. SHAP concentration achieves $\rho = 0.833$ ($p = 0.010$) with coverage drop; MMD achieves $\rho = -0.048$ ($p = 0.91$), C2ST $\rho = 0.191$ ($p = 0.65$), and PSI $\rho = 0.071$ ($p = 0.87$). Mann-Whitney tests show no metric reaches group-separation significance at $\alpha=0.05$; SHAP concentration is borderline ($p = 0.057$; MMD $p = 0.63$, C2ST $p = 0.27$, PSI $p = 0.63$). This binary group-separation test is secondary at $n=8$; the primary endpoint throughout is rank correlation magnitude.

\subsection{Retraining Analysis}

We test four retraining frequencies on catastrophic (sales-shipcond) and robust (sales-office) tasks over 11 months (Feb--Dec 2020), then extend quarterly retraining to all three severe tasks to assess whether retraining effectiveness depends on task complexity.

\textbf{Sales-shipcond} (45 classes; Table~\ref{tab:retrain}, Figure~\ref{fig:retrain}): Quarterly retraining achieves the highest mean coverage (41.1\%), a statistically significant improvement of +18.9~pp over no retraining (Wilcoxon $p=0.04$). Monthly retraining shows higher variance and occasional collapse (min: 0.6\%). Quarterly offers the best cost-effectiveness: 3 retrains/year achieves 85\% of maximum improvement.

\textbf{Sales-group} (462 classes): Quarterly retraining barely helps---3-month mean coverage is 2.2\% vs.\ 0.0\% baseline. The class space is too large for the model to recover meaningful calibration from limited post-shift data. In this setting, when class cardinality overwhelms the retraining signal, neither retraining nor RAPS (Section~\ref{sec:raps}) restored useful coverage.

\textbf{Sales-payterms} (135 classes): Partial recovery---3-month mean coverage reaches 28.1\% vs.\ 12.6\% baseline (+15.5~pp). The moderate class count allows some recalibration but remains far below the 90\% target.

\textbf{Robust task}: Coverage remains 99.8--99.9\% regardless of retraining frequency, suggesting limited retraining benefit in this setting.

\textbf{Synthesis}: Retraining effectiveness depends on task complexity. For moderate class counts ($\leq$45), quarterly retraining provides meaningful recovery (+19~pp). For very large class spaces (462), retraining is essentially ineffective. This gradient---from partial recovery (sales-shipcond, sales-payterms) to failure (sales-group)---strengthens the case for pre-deployment diagnostics: identifying vulnerable tasks enables appropriate mitigation rather than uniform retraining.

\textbf{Multi-seed assessment.} Under a strict threshold-transfer lens, 7/9 external domains are deterministic or near-deterministic (6 deterministic, 1 near-deterministic): Covertype, Gas Sensor, Avila, PAMAP2, Satimage, Pendigits (all 10/10), and Shuttle (9/10). The two variable cases are KDDCup99 (intermediate regime) and Stack Overflow (near-binary ceiling mismatch).

\textbf{Combined correlation.} Restricting to multiclass tasks ($n=16$, 9 domains): $\rho = 0.853$, $p < 0.001$ (Kendall $\tau = 0.667$, $p < 0.001$; bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.50, 0.96]$). Including binary ceiling-effect tasks ($n=19$, 11 domains): $\rho = 0.814$, $p < 0.001$. Relative to SALT-only ($\rho = 0.833$, $p = 0.010$, $n=8$), cross-domain evidence increases sample size and precision while exposing intermediate-regime exceptions (notably KDDCup99).

\textbf{Binary ceiling effect.} The three binary tasks show near-zero coverage drops ($-1.3\%$, $+2.9\%$, $+1.2\%$) despite SHAP concentrations spanning $20.8\%$ to $48.1\%$. This reflects a \textit{structural} property of binary APS: prediction sets are restricted to $\{0\}$, $\{1\}$, or $\{0,1\}$, leaving little room for coverage degradation. Mean binary drop $= 0.9\%$ vs.\ multiclass $= 33.6\%$ (Mann-Whitney $p = 0.024$). Binary APS is structurally protected against the failure mode that concentration detects.

\textbf{COVID-era subset.} Restricting to the 9 COVID-era tasks (8 SALT + study-outcome) yields $\rho = 0.883$, $p = 0.002$, suggesting SHAP concentration is particularly diagnostic under severe, event-driven shift.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The Covertype result is especially informative: it demonstrates the diagnostic works in the ``hard direction''---correctly predicting catastrophic failure for a high-concentration task in a completely different domain (ecological remote sensing vs.\ supply chain), fully deterministic across 10 seeds. KDDCup99 provides the complementary boundary case: despite low mean concentration ($C=21.1\%$), coverage drop is highly seed-dependent (mean $15.9\pm21.4$~pp), indicating that intermediate regimes can violate a simple threshold rule even when concentration is low on average.

\textbf{Asymmetric evidence structure.} Among 8 external multiclass datasets, Covertype is the only clearly catastrophic case and KDDCup99 is the only intermediate-regime at-risk case missed by the 40\% threshold. The remaining 6 are robust and correctly unflagged. This asymmetry reflects a property of public benchmarks: most standard datasets distribute importance across features (median external $C = 19.2\%$), making catastrophic single-feature dependence the exception rather than the rule. A targeted search for additional high-concentration catastrophic candidates (CWRU Bearing Fault, 10 classes, cross-load shift) yielded $C = 29.9\%$---below the 40\% threshold and robust by observed coverage. The diagnostic's value remains asymmetric: catastrophic failures are rare but costly, while intermediate regimes (like KDDCup99) motivate conservative monitoring even when $C$ is low.

\subsection{Placebo Test}

To establish that COVID-19 represents a unique shift, we use pre-COVID data: train on 2018, validate on 2019-H1, test on 2019-H2 (Table~\ref{tab:placebo} in Appendix). COVID causes 6--140$\times$ more degradation than normal temporal drift for vulnerable tasks. This confirms that the observed coverage collapse is attributable to COVID-19 disruption rather than routine temporal drift, and that the SHAP concentration diagnostic identifies vulnerability specific to the severe shift regime.

\section{Decision Framework}\label{sec:framework}

The following exploratory protocol, empirically motivated by our analysis, can guide practitioners deploying conformal prediction in non-stationary settings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Compute SHAP concentration} $C = \phi_1 / \sum_j \phi_j$ on validation data (Eq.~\ref{eq:concentration})
    \item \textbf{If $C \leq 40\%$}: lower observed risk in this study (distributed importance may protect against shift)
    \item \textbf{If $C > 40\%$}: Check for protective factors---secondary features with train-validation Jaccard $> 0.5$ \textit{and} importance $> 15\%$ (validation overlap serves as a pre-deployment proxy for deployment-time feature stability)
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Protective factors exist $\rightarrow$ \textbf{robust}
        \item Otherwise $\rightarrow$ \textbf{vulnerable}: consider quarterly retraining
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{For lower-risk tasks}: defer routine retraining (saves computational cost)
    \item \textbf{Monitor}: Track empirical coverage as early warning
\end{enumerate}

\noindent\textbf{Seed stability protocol.} KDDCup99's multi-seed analysis ($C=21.1\pm7.5\%$) reveals that single-seed SHAP concentration can be unstable. We recommend computing $C$ over $k \geq 5$ seeds and using the mean. Tasks in the ambiguous zone (35--45\%) should receive additional scrutiny: if the upper 95\% CI exceeds 40\%, treat as potentially vulnerable. For SALT tasks, concentration is stable across 50 seeds (within-task CV $<$ 5\%); instability arises primarily when the top-feature identity varies across seeds.

\noindent\textbf{Threshold selection.} The 40\% threshold is proposed based on the empirical distribution of SHAP concentration values on validation data: a natural gap separates low-concentration tasks (24--29\%) from high-concentration tasks (43--54\%), visible in Table~\ref{tab:framework_validation}. We emphasize that this threshold is \textit{exploratory}, derived from $n=8$ multiclass tasks, and should be validated before deployment in new domains.

\noindent\textbf{Cross-domain transfer test.} The 40\% threshold applied without tuning to 9 external domains yields 7/9 deterministic or near-deterministic outcomes (6 at 10/10, Shuttle at 9/10), with Covertype correctly flagged as catastrophic and Gas Sensor correctly unflagged as robust. KDDCup99 is the principal intermediate-regime false negative, while Stack Overflow (3 classes) exhibits the near-binary ceiling effect. Across the $n=16$ multiclass set: $\rho = 0.853$, $p < 0.001$ (Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain}). The sensitivity analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:threshold} reports threshold performance across 30--50\%; given sparse catastrophic external cases, this threshold should be treated as provisional.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Confidently Wrong Models.} Counter-intuitively, catastrophic tasks show \textit{smaller} prediction sets at test time (\eg, sales-shipcond: 7.0 $\rightarrow$ 3.0). The model concentrates probability on a small number of wrong classes rather than spreading uncertainty. This is mechanistically consistent with our theory: when the model depends on a single OOD feature, it maps unseen values to arbitrary but \textit{concentrated} probability distributions. Appendix~\ref{app:baselines} confirms that prediction entropy \textit{decreases} for catastrophic tasks---the opposite of what entropy-based monitoring would flag.

\textbf{High Variance as Warning.} Tasks with CV $> 50\%$ across seeds are in knife-edge regimes. This is itself an early warning that conformal calibration will be unreliable.

\textbf{Shift Detection is Necessary but Not Sufficient.} MMD and C2ST correctly identify that COVID-19 induces distributional shift for all 8 tasks---but this is precisely why they are uninformative as diagnostics: shift is widespread, failure is not. All tasks show shift signal, yet coverage drops range from 0.1\% to 77\%. SHAP concentration helps resolve this by measuring the model's \textit{dependence structure}: not ``is there shift?'' but ``is this model likely to amplify shift into conformal failure?'' This distinction is critical for practitioners deciding which models to retrain.

\textbf{Alternative Conformal Methods.} Mondrian conformal prediction~\citep{vovk2005algorithmic} calibrates per-class, but when the dominant feature undergoes complete shift (Jaccard $\approx 0$), test-time values can lack calibration counterparts. Weighted conformal prediction~\citep{tibshirani2019conformal} faces the same obstacle when overlap assumptions are weak. RAPS~\citep{angelopoulos2021uncertainty} helps many-class tasks through regularization but does not fix the fundamental exchangeability violation (Section~\ref{sec:raps}). SHAP concentration describes the \textit{fixed model's} learned dependence structure (computed on validation data), rather than test-distribution overlap, so it remains informative in settings where overlap-based corrections are limited.

\textbf{Limitations.}

These results provide strong associative evidence in the studied settings, not universal causal guarantees.

\textbf{Generalizability.} (1) The core SALT analysis (8 tasks) shares a single database, temporal split, and model class. Cross-domain extension to 16 multiclass tasks across 9 domains mitigates this: $\rho = 0.853$, $p < 0.001$ (bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.50, 0.96]$; Kendall $\tau = 0.667$), spanning 7 types of natural distribution shift, with external validation confirming catastrophic and robust regimes plus intermediate-case caveats (Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain}). (2) The 40\% threshold is exploratory, derived from $n=8$ multiclass tasks. External validation on 9 held-out domains shows deterministic/near-deterministic behavior for 7/9 domains, with KDDCup99 and Stack Overflow as boundary cases; only one external dataset is clearly catastrophic, so external sensitivity estimates remain uncertain. (3) Binary APS tasks exhibit a structural ceiling effect (Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain}) that limits both the failure mode and the diagnostic's applicability; the concentration diagnostic is validated for multiclass settings only. (4) Threshold performance is evaluated retrospectively using held-out outcomes (drop $>$15~pp), not through a prospective live deployment trial.

\textbf{Methodology.} (5) The 50 random seeds share identical training and test data, varying only in model randomness and calibration splits. ICC analysis (Appendix~\ref{app:icc}) confirms task identity explains most variance (ICC $= 0.675$, effective $n_{\text{eff}} = 11.7$), and per-task negative ICC(val,test) indicates paired tests are conservative. After Holm--Bonferroni correction for 5 concentration metrics tested, the SALT-only top-1 result reaches $p_{\text{adj}} = 0.050$ (Appendix~\ref{app:icc}), so evidence is borderline under this conservative correction. The multiclass cross-domain primary endpoint ($n=16$) remains strongly significant ($\rho = 0.853$, $p < 0.001$). (6) Among pre-deployment diagnostics (Section~\ref{sec:baselines}), SHAP concentration achieves stronger correlation than native feature importance ($\rho = 0.667$, $p = 0.071$) and ensemble disagreement ($\rho = 0.452$, $p = 0.260$), though the difference between dependent correlations is not formally testable at $n=8$.

\textbf{Scope.} (7) The diagnostic is model-specific: all three gradient-boosted implementations show positive correlation (LGB $\rho = 0.833$, CatBoost $\rho = 0.667$, XGB $\rho = 0.548$) while RandomForest does not replicate ($\rho = 0.30$; Appendix~\ref{app:rf}). MLP replication across all 8 tasks ($\rho = 0.43$, $p = 0.29$; Appendix~\ref{app:rf}) confirms model-specificity but shows the diagnostic is not predictive for neural networks, where failure mechanisms differ from concentrated single-feature dependence. A mixed-effects model accounting for task-level clustering confirms the concentration effect across boosting models ($\beta_1 = 1.64$, $p = 0.0006$, $n=24$), but each model has different task-level vulnerability profiles, so the diagnostic must be computed for the deployed model class. (8) The protective-factor thresholds (Jaccard $> 0.5$, importance $> 15\%$) are preliminary heuristics validated on 1 example. (9) Theorem~\ref{thm:score_inflation} provides a rigorous score-inflation bound under an additive decomposition model; the bound is verified on 5/5 applicable tasks (Section~\ref{sec:theory}), though the $(K{-}1)$ rank-counting step introduces looseness for large $K$.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We propose SHAP concentration as a pre-deployment diagnostic for conformal prediction vulnerability under distribution shift. Across 16 multiclass tasks in 9 domains, evidence in this study indicates:
(1) Coverage drops range from negligible ($<$1\%) to catastrophic ($>$80\%) under distribution shift; SALT task-level drops are all significant under paired tests;
(2) SHAP concentration correlates with which tasks fail ($\rho=0.853$, $p < 0.001$; Kendall $\tau = 0.667$; bootstrap 95\% CI $[0.50, 0.96]$);
(3) external validation across 9 held-out domains supports partial transfer, with 7/9 deterministic-or-near-deterministic threshold outcomes across 10 seeds;
(4) Theorem~\ref{thm:score_inflation} provides a rigorous score-inflation bound that is monotone in concentration, verified empirically on 5/5 applicable tasks and supported by conformity score stochastic dominance (KS $= 0.68$--$0.96$ for catastrophic tasks);
(5) standard shift detectors (MMD, C2ST) detect shift for all tasks uniformly but cannot distinguish catastrophic from robust outcomes ($\rho \leq 0.22$), whereas SHAP concentration shows the strongest observed severity association among evaluated pre-deployment metrics ($\rho = 0.833$, $p = 0.010$);
(6) binary APS is structurally protected against this failure mode regardless of concentration;
(7) quarterly retraining partially mitigates vulnerable coverage loss by +19~pp ($p=0.04$);
(8) ACI recovers coverage for vulnerable tasks but inflates sets to 30--82\% of classes for those needing intervention; robust tasks show limited ACI benefit in this setting.

The diagnostic is pre-deployment (validation data only), potentially actionable (retrain/skip decision), and operationally stable in this study, with transparent precision--recall trade-offs across 30--45\% thresholds.
Given retrospective threshold evaluation and sparse catastrophic external cases, prospective deployment validation remains an important next step before policy-level adoption.

More broadly, our results suggest that the \textit{structure} of learned feature importance---not only the magnitude of distribution shift---is strongly associated with whether conformal guarantees degrade in practice. Standard shift detection answers ``is there shift?'' (yes, often); SHAP concentration addresses the practitioner's question: ``is \textit{my model} likely to fail under this shift?'' This distinction opens a direction for pre-deployment model auditing based on importance concentration analysis.

\subsection{Conformal Prediction}

Method: APS~\citep{romano2020classification}. Target coverage: $(1-\alpha)=0.9$ (90\%). Calibration: random 50\% split of validation set. Quantile: $q = \min(\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil/n, 1)$.

\noindent\textbf{Calibration and test set sizes.} Sales-level tasks (s-shipcond, s-payterms, s-group, s-incoterms, s-office): $n_{\text{val}} = 71{,}474$, $n_{\text{cal}} = 35{,}737$, $n_{\text{test}} = 88{,}942$. At these calibration set sizes, the finite-sample correction is negligible: $q - (1-\alpha) < 3 \times 10^{-5}$ for all tasks, so finite-sample effects do not contribute to the observed coverage gaps.

\subsection{SHAP Computation}

Method: TreeExplainer~\citep{lundberg2020local}. Subsample: 10,000 per split. Aggregation: mean absolute SHAP across classes. Concentration metric (Eq.~\ref{eq:concentration}): computed on validation (pre-COVID) data only.

\subsection{Baseline Diagnostics}

Native feature importance concentration uses a single model (seed=42) with LightGBM's gain-based importance; concentration is top-1 gain divided by total gain. Ensemble disagreement is the standard deviation of validation coverage across 50 seeds. Both are computed without SHAP.

\subsection{Computational Resources}

Standard CPU (8 cores, 8GB RAM). Wall-clock: $\sim$3--4 hours for full 8-task, 50-seed suite. Software: Python 3.9, LightGBM 3.3, SHAP 0.41.

\section{Task Independence Analysis}\label{app:icc}

We analyze pseudo-replication in the 50-seed design using intraclass correlation (ICC).

\textbf{Between-task ICC.} For coverage drops, ICC $= 0.675$ (95\% CI: $[0.47, 0.90]$; $F(7,392)=104.6$), confirming that task identity explains the majority of seed-level variance. The design effect is 34.1, yielding effective $n_{\text{eff}} = 400/34.1 = 11.7$---exceeding the 8 task-level observations. For test coverages, ICC $= 0.720$ (95\% CI: $[0.52, 0.92]$). These values confirm that the $n=8$ tasks constitute meaningfully independent observations for the Spearman correlation analysis.

\textbf{Per-task ICC(val,test).} For each task, we compute ICC between the 50 validation coverages and 50 test coverages (paired by seed). All 8 per-task ICCs are negative ($-0.80$ to $-0.10$), indicating that seeds producing above-average validation coverage do \textit{not} produce above-average test coverage. This means paired Wilcoxon tests are conservative: the effective sample size for within-task paired tests \textit{exceeds} 50, so the reported $p$-values are, if anything, too large.

\textbf{Results.} The diagnostic's predictive power varies by model class (Table~\ref{tab:model_sensitivity}). All three gradient-boosted implementations show positive correlation: LightGBM ($\rho = 0.833$, $p = 0.010$), CatBoost ($\rho = 0.667$, $p = 0.071$), and XGBoost ($\rho = 0.548$, $p = 0.16$). RandomForest ($\rho = 0.30$, $p = 0.47$) does not replicate.

\textbf{Mixed-effects analysis.} Since (task, model) pairs share data and temporal split, we fit a linear mixed-effects model with task as a random intercept to properly account for clustering: $\text{Drop}_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{Concentration}_{ij} + u_i + \varepsilon_{ij}$, where $u_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_u)$ is the task random effect. Across the three gradient-boosted models ($n=24$, 8 tasks $\times$ 3 models), the concentration effect is $\beta_1 = 1.64$ (95\% CI $[0.70, 2.58]$, $p = 0.0006$): each 1\% increase in concentration predicts 1.6~pp additional coverage drop, after accounting for task-level clustering. Including model class as a fixed effect yields $\beta_1 = 1.66$ ($p = 0.001$). With all four models including RF ($n=32$): $\beta_1 = 1.06$ ($p = 0.010$).

\textbf{Interpretation.} All three gradient-boosted implementations show the concentration--drop relationship, but with different vulnerability profiles. CatBoost's ordered boosting produces dramatically different task-level vulnerabilities: LGB-catastrophic tasks (s-shipcond: 71.6\% LGB drop) show only 1.1\% CatBoost drop, while i-shippoint reverses (18.5\% LGB $\rightarrow$ 61.8\% CatBoost). This confirms each model's SHAP concentration captures its \textit{own} learned dependence---not a universal data property---and that cross-model transfer is unreliable (LGB conc.\ $\rightarrow$ CatBoost drop: $\rho = 0.07$). RF's bagging produces smoother probability surfaces, compressing both concentration range and vulnerability profiles. Specifically, RF's failure to replicate ($\rho = 0.30$) has two mechanistic causes: (1)~\textit{compressed concentration range}---RF concentrations span 17.5--57.4\% vs.\ LGB's 23.7--54.2\%, but the RF range is driven by a single outlier (s-shipcond at 57.4\%); excluding it, the RF range is 17.5--26.8\%, offering almost no discriminative signal; (2)~\textit{smoothed probability surfaces}---bagging averages 500 independent trees, each seeing random feature subsets, which distributes effective dependence more evenly than boosting's sequential error-correction. The result is that RF rarely develops the concentrated single-feature dependence that drives catastrophic conformal failure in boosting models, and consequently SHAP concentration has limited predictive power for RF vulnerability.

The diagnostic is \textit{model-specific}: it measures how a particular model's learned dependence structure creates vulnerability. Practitioners should compute concentration for their \textit{deployed} model class rather than transferring results across model families.

\textbf{Neural network extension.} To test beyond tree-based models, we evaluate a 2-layer MLP (128/64 hidden units, ReLU, softmax, permutation importance) on all 8 SALT tasks (10 seeds for sales-level tasks, 3 for item-level). The within-MLP concentration--drop correlation is $\rho = 0.43$ ($p = 0.29$), similar to RF and not significant. However, the model-specificity claim is strikingly confirmed across all 8 tasks: the MLP learns fundamentally different concentration profiles from tree-based models. Key examples: (1)~i-shippoint shows MLP $C=77.2\%$, drop $= 82.3$~pp vs.\ LGB $C=48.8\%$, drop $= 18.5$~pp---the MLP concentrates nearly all importance on a single feature and fails catastrophically, while LGB distributes importance and maintains coverage; (2)~s-incoterms shows MLP $C=30.3\%$, drop $= 42.3$~pp vs.\ LGB $C=23.7\%$, drop $= 8.5$~pp---the MLP's greater concentration predicts its greater failure; (3)~s-office shows MLP $C \approx 0\%$, drop $= 0.1$~pp---perfect robustness prediction. The non-significant within-MLP $\rho$ is driven by two tasks (s-group: $C=27.5\%$, drop $= 78.4$~pp; s-payterms: $C=28.0\%$, drop $= 60.6$~pp) where the MLP distributes importance evenly yet still fails catastrophically---suggesting the MLP's failure mechanism involves global sensitivity to the shift rather than concentrated single-feature dependence. This confirms that SHAP concentration is diagnostic specifically for the concentrated-dependence failure mode that characterizes gradient-boosted models, not a universal predictor of all failure modes.
